Enhancing Program Integrity and Integrity and Interagency Coordination in the Administration of the H-1B Nonimmigrant Visa Program

The H-1B nonimmigrant visa program has operated since 1990 under rules set by Congress that let U.S. employers bring in foreign workers for up to six years in specialty occupations that require at least a bachelor's degree in a specific field. The requires employers to pay those workers the higher of the actual wage paid to similar workers at the company or the prevailing wage for the occupation in the area, and it bars the use of H-1B labor to displace qualified U.S. workers. Over the past decade federal agencies have conducted audits and investigations that identified patterns of wage underpayment, misuse of the program for lower-skilled roles, and outsourcing arrangements that move work overseas after initial placement.

On September 18, 2026, President Trump signed an executive order titled Enhancing Program Integrity and Interagency Coordination in the Administration of the H-1B Nonimmigrant Visa Program. The order directs multiple cabinet departments and agencies to tighten enforcement of existing wage and qualification rules, expand data sharing across agencies on visa petitions and labor-condition applications, and strengthen site visits and audits of employers and third-party contractors that file large volumes of H-1B requests.

The order changes how agencies handle petitions by requiring faster interagency referrals when discrepancies appear between claimed wages, job duties, and actual worksite conditions. It places new emphasis on coordinated reviews of outsourcing firms that have accounted for more than 25,000 cap registrations in fiscal year 2026 and on employers that have conducted mass layoffs while continuing to sponsor H-1B workers. The situation now turns on the extent to which the directed enforcement actions and information exchanges alter approval rates and compliance behavior among the largest users of the program.

Constitutional analysis
The federal government exists first to secure the rights of American citizens, including their ability to earn a living in peace under laws made with their consent. The H-1B program was meant by statute to bring in only uniquely skilled foreign workers when no qualified American could be found. Instead, outsourcing firms and certain employers have used it to replace American technical workers with lower-paid visa holders, driving down wages by an estimated $9,000 to $20,000 in affected fields and prompting mass layoffs followed by immediate H-1B hiring. The executive order issued September 18, 2026, directs agencies to tighten program integrity and improve coordination to stop these documented abuses. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to establish a uniform rule of naturalization and to regulate commerce with foreign nations. The founders viewed immigration and labor policy as tools to strengthen the new republic, not to weaken the bargaining power of its own citizens. In the 1790s, Congress passed the first naturalization acts and tariff laws to protect domestic industry and encourage American skill. Washington himself called out the militia in 1794 to enforce federal law when revenue collection was threatened. The same principle of energetic but limited federal action applies here: the executive must enforce the statutory limits on H-1B use so the program serves its original additive purpose. Federalism and enumerated powers reinforce the point. States retain primary authority over domestic labor markets and the general welfare of their residents under the Tenth Amendment. When federal visa rules are gamed to flood those markets with cheaper foreign labor, they undermine the consent of the governed and the domestic tranquility the Preamble names as a core reason for government. The order’s focus on interagency coordination and fraud detection restores the original boundary between temporary foreign supplementation and permanent displacement of American workers.
Thinking ahead
The executive order directs federal agencies to tighten checks on H-1B visa approvals, increase site visits, share data across departments, and prioritize fraud probes against outsourcing firms that dominate the top registration slots. It builds on years of agency findings that many approvals go to lower-wage placements rather than the uniquely skilled temporary workers the program was written for in 1990. The order sets deadlines for new interagency rules, enforcement plans, and reports back to the White House. This step will likely run through the standard rulemaking timeline of 12 to 18 months before the first binding changes take hold, assuming no court injunctions block implementation. Next moves hinge on how quickly agencies issue proposed rules, how many public comments they draw, and whether career staff complete the required wage and skill audits on the top outsourcing users that filed over 25,000 registrations in fiscal 2026. If lawsuits from industry groups stall the process, the timeline stretches; if audits turn up clear fraud patterns, enforcement actions could start sooner on specific employers. The situation changes only when new approval data shows a measurable drop in outsourcing-heavy registrations or when agencies publish the first compliance reports. Horizon: 12–18 months to first operational rules.
