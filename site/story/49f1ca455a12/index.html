Justice Department seeks $61 million in what it says is ill-gotten Iranian cryptocurrency

The United States and Israel began strikes on Iran in late February 2026. The fighting settled into weekly exchanges by early March, with naval clashes in the Strait of Hormuz. A June memorandum lifted the dual blockades and opened a 60-day negotiating window. That window collapsed on July 8, and sustained US strikes resumed. The conflict narrowed to control of the strait. Iran has sold crude oil on the black market to generate during the fighting.

On September 15 the Justice Department filed a civil complaint in New York federal court for $61 million in cryptocurrency. The filing accuses the Iranian military, including the Islamic Revolutionary Guard , of acquiring the funds through black-market oil sales. It two Chinese companies laundered the revenue through trading accounts at a cryptocurrency exchange in the United Arab Emirates, routing it through a network of unhosted addresses that moved roughly $1.5 billion from those sales.

The complaint places the seized cryptocurrency under court review for potential forfeiture to the United States. It stands as one more US effort to cut off revenue streams that have continued while the two sides contest the strait.

The Justice Department filed a civil forfeiture complaint in New York federal court on September 15 for $61 million in cryptocurrency tied to Iranian oil sales routed through two Chinese firms and a UAE exchange. This is the latest US move in the running fight over the Strait of Hormuz that began with strikes in late February, broke down after the June memorandum collapsed in July, and has since focused on blocking Iranian revenue streams. The complaint names the IRGC and alleges the funds came from black-market crude that bypassed sanctions. Next step is a court review of the evidence; if the judge agrees the money is proceeds of crime, the US seizes it. Iran has shown no sign of halting oil sales or crypto transfers in the seven weeks since the last major strike cycle, so the forfeiture fight is likely to drag on through more complaints and frozen wallets until either side reaches a new diplomatic pause or the battlefield situation around Hormuz changes sharply. This runs at least another three to six months.
