WASHINGTON
September 29, 2026 · 21:21 UTC

Trump signed an executive order to launch the AI-powered America.gov portal in Washington ahead of a meeting with AI executives

The executive branch has used the term artificial since at least 2019 when President Trump issued the first order directing federal agencies to invest in AI research and development. That 2019 order created the American AI Initiative which required agencies to prioritize federal data and computing resources for AI systems and to set standards for their use across government. Subsequent administrations kept the term in place while expanding funding and oversight.

On September 29 2026 President Trump signed an executive order that directs every federal agency to strike the phrase artificial intelligence from all documents regulations and programs and replace it with super intelligence. The order applies only inside the executive branch and does not change or private-sector usage.

The government now operates under the new label for its technology investments procurement rules and interagency coordination. Every reference in existing executive-branch guidance must be updated to match the order.

Constitutional analysis
President Donald J. Trump signed an executive order on September 29 directing the executive branch to replace the term “Artificial Intelligence” with “Super Intelligence” in all official documents and communications. The order claims this change recognizes the advancing technological frontier and the economic benefits it can deliver to Americans. Article II, Section 1 of the Constitution vests executive power in the president, and Article II, Section 3 requires him to “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed.” The order does not cite any specific statute that uses the term “Artificial Intelligence” or grants the president power to rewrite terminology across the entire executive branch. The founders debated the scope of executive authority in The Federalist No. 69 and No. 77, where Hamilton explained that the president’s power stops short of legislative functions reserved to Congress. The first Congress passed the Decision of 1789, affirming that the president could remove executive officers but leaving policy terminology to statutes. A statute or congressional amendment would settle the boundary between executive housekeeping and legislative definition of terms. The order reflects the president’s view that the United States leads in these technologies and should name them accordingly.
Thinking ahead
President Trump signed an executive order on September 29 directing the federal government to swap the term Artificial Intelligence for Super Intelligence in all executive branch documents and communications. The order frames the name change as a way to signal that the technology has moved past its earlier stage and now carries broader economic potential for the US. No funding, new regulations, or agency reorganizations were announced with it. The change sits inside the executive branch only, so it binds federal agencies and contractors working with them but leaves Congress, courts, and private industry free to keep using the old term. Past name shifts in tech policy, such as the move from machine learning to AI in grant language around 2015, took roughly 18 to 24 months to ripple through most federal paperwork before new habits stuck. Next step is the Office of Management and Budget issuing guidance on how agencies rewrite existing rules, forms, and websites. The order lasts as long as the current administration holds the White House; a later president could reverse it with another order. Without follow-on spending bills or legislation that codifies the term in statute, the practical effect stays limited to branding inside the executive branch. This naming directive is likely to run its course inside one to two budget cycles unless Congress writes the new term into law.
