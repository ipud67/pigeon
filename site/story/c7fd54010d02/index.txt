17:T622,The Constitution grants Congress power to lay and collect taxes under Article I, Section 8, Clause 1, but only for the common Defense and general Welfare of the United States—not to pick winners in Hollywood or any private industry. President Trump’s call for a federal tax incentive to lure film and TV production back to California, backed by Democrats Newsom and Schiff, treats tax policy as a tool for industrial favoritism rather than uniform revenue for legitimate federal ends. The founders designed taxes to fund enumerated objects like defense and courts, not to steer private investment or compete with state incentives; Hamilton in Federalist No. 21 warned against taxes that distort commerce or invite crony influence. The Tariff Act of 1789 set uniform duties to protect revenue and navigation, not to subsidize one sector over another. This move stretches the General Welfare Clause beyond the limited, enumerated powers the framers debated in the First Bank fight and McCulloch, where even broad construction still required a clear tie to enumerated authority. States like California remain free to offer their own credits under the Tenth Amendment, but turning Washington into a national arts patron inverts the founding order: government exists first to secure life, liberty, and property so free enterprise—including movies—can flourish without federal direction.
