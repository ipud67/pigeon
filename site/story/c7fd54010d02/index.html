Newsom, Schiff back Trump’s push for federal film tax credit

In 2017, California extended its own state film and television tax credit program, which gives qualified productions a credit against state taxes for spending on wages, sets and other in-state costs. The program has operated since 2009 and caps the total credits available each year.

On September 1, 2025, President Trump posted on Truth Social calling on Congress to create a new federal tax credit for film and TV production shot in the United States.

The next day, California Governor Gavin Newsom and Senator Adam Schiff both publicly backed the president’s call for the federal incentive.

The measure has not yet been introduced as legislation.

Constitutional analysis
The Constitution grants Congress power to lay and collect taxes under Article I, Section 8, Clause 1, but only for the common Defense and general Welfare of the United States—not to pick winners in Hollywood or any private industry. President Trump’s call for a federal tax incentive to lure film and TV production back to California, backed by Democrats Newsom and Schiff, treats tax policy as a tool for industrial favoritism rather than uniform revenue for legitimate federal ends. The founders designed taxes to fund enumerated objects like defense and courts, not to steer private investment or compete with state incentives; Hamilton in Federalist No. 21 warned against taxes that distort commerce or invite crony influence. The Tariff Act of 1789 set uniform duties to protect revenue and navigation, not to subsidize one sector over another. This move stretches the General Welfare Clause beyond the limited, enumerated powers the framers debated in the First Bank fight and McCulloch, where even broad construction still required a clear tie to enumerated authority. States like California remain free to offer their own credits under the Tenth Amendment, but turning Washington into a national arts patron inverts the founding order: government exists first to secure life, liberty, and property so free enterprise—including movies—can flourish without federal direction. Bipartisan backing does not cure the defect; the consent of the governed demands taxes stay tied to true public purposes, not to bring jobs or glamour to one industry or one state.
Predictive model
Newsom and Schiff, both California Democrats, lined up behind Trump’s call for a new federal tax credit that would pull film and TV shoots back from overseas. The move marks the latest step in a long-running fight over production jobs that have been draining out of Hollywood for years. Next concrete rung is whether Congress actually writes and votes on a bill; that would need House and Senate majorities plus a presidential signature. The issue stays alive as long as studios keep shifting work abroad and California keeps losing sound-stage hours. If no bill reaches the floor by the end of the current Congress, the push dies until new sponsors pick it up again. Watch for committee hearings or a formal legislative draft as the first measurable sign it is moving forward. Bipartisan backing could speed things up, but without floor action it stays talk. Horizon: 6–12 months before a bill either passes or quietly drops.
