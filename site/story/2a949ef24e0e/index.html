Biologists discover new tiger cat species in Bolivia: the tilcayo

Translated from Dutch · read the original →
The original headlineBiologen ontdekken nieuw soort tijgerkat in Bolivia: de tilcayo

The last new species of small wild cat entered the scientific record in 1929. Since then, taxonomists have relied on camera traps, DNA samples, and museum skins to reclassify known cats rather than add new ones. In the high forests of the Bolivian Andes, a distinct population of tiger cat has lived apart from its lowland relatives for hundreds of thousands of years, separated by altitude and habitat.

On September 17, 2026, a team of biologists published the formal description of that population in the journal Current Biology. They named it the tilcayo, a small spotted feline with a coat pattern and skull shape that set it apart from the oncilla and the northern tiger cat already on the books. Genetic analysis and field observations confirmed it as a separate species.

The discovery adds one more named mammal to the Andean list and shifts the known range of tiger cats upward into cloud-forest terrain that had been surveyed only lightly. Future surveys will now treat the tilcayo as its own conservation unit rather than a variant of existing cats.

Thinking ahead
Biologists in Bolivia found a new small wild cat species called the tilcayo in the Andes and published the description in the journal Current Biology on September 17 2026. This marks the first new feline species recorded in 100 years. The find rests on physical specimens camera trap images and DNA samples collected over years in high elevation forests. The next procedural step is formal taxonomic acceptance by the International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature which reviews the published evidence against existing cat classifications. Further field surveys will map the tilcayo’s exact range and population size since current data cover only a few sites. The situation changes only when new specimens or genetic sequences contradict the original description or when habitat loss in the Andes alters the cat’s known distribution. This story will stay in scientific channels for the next 12 to 24 months while taxonomists and conservation teams test and expand the record. Horizon: acceptance and range map by late 2027.
