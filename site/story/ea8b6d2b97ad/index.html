United Kingdom’s government says Foreign Secretary spoke at the UN Security Council on peace and security in Ukraine

The United Nations Security Council was created in 1945 to handle threats to international peace and security. Over the past decade governments have increasingly discussed how new technologies fit inside that mandate. Artificial entered those talks in 2018 when the council held its first formal meeting on the subject. Since then member states have traded proposals on norms for military uses of AI while private labs raced ahead on model development.

On 23 September 2026 the United Kingdom’s Foreign Secretary told the council that governments must not leave frontier AI model safety to the companies that build them. She listed three concrete steps: rigorous testing of the largest models before release, mandatory visibility for governments into how those models are trained and updated, and hardened national defenses against misuse of AI in cyber attacks on or financial systems. She pointed to the AI Security Institute the UK opened in 2023 as the mechanism already sharing test results with the United States and major labs.

The council session ended with no new resolution or binding commitment. The UK said it will carry the same agenda into its G20 presidency in 2027. What governments actually require of AI developers and how those requirements will be verified remain the open questions the council left on the table.

Thinking ahead
The UK foreign secretary told the UN Security Council that governments must take the lead on AI safety, transparency, and resilience instead of leaving it to private labs. He pointed to the first AI Security Institute the UK set up, its work with US labs, and the coming UK G20 presidency as the next steps in that effort. The speech follows repeated warnings from AI founders that current private efforts alone cannot prevent major harm. This push sits inside the council’s pattern of meetings on new tech risks. The procedural next rung is continued working groups and bilateral deals on model testing standards. Governments would need to pass binding rules on frontier model access and shared incident reporting for the posture to shift from voluntary pledges to enforced oversight. The call for resilience measures — hardened cyber defenses, protected infrastructure, workforce adjustment programs — sets up parallel national and allied efforts that run alongside the UN track. The situation stays on this voluntary-cooperation ladder until a major tested model release or a verified AI-enabled breach forces tighter rules. Horizon: Another 12–18 months of institute-level testing pacts and G20 talks before any council resolution is likely.
