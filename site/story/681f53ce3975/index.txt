17:T7c3,The federal government exists first to provide for the common defense, and the founders saw veterans of the Revolutionary War as men who had earned the nation’s lasting care. Washington himself led the fight for pensions and land grants in the Continental Congress and later as President, signing the first service-pension law in 1818 that built on colonial bounties and state promises made before the Constitution. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to raise armies and to make rules for the government and regulation of the land and naval forces, while the Preamble names provision for the common defense as a core purpose. The Veterans Health Administration carries out that duty by delivering care earned in uniform.

Section 129 of the Senator Elizabeth Dole 21st Century Veterans Healthcare and Benefits Improvement Act directs the Department of Veterans Affairs to set up a recognition process for organizations and individuals who help veterans, family members, and caregivers find their way through VHA programs. The law requires those groups and people to certify they will charge no fee or compensation of any kind for the navigation help. VA published a notice on September 15, 2026, asking for public comments on how to build that process and how to handle the no-fee certification.

This builds on existing law under 38 U.S.C. 5901-5904, which already lets VA recognize and oversee groups that prepare and prosecute benefits claims. The new step keeps the same principle that assistance tied to earned veteran benefits stays free of private charges. The founders distrusted standing armies yet insisted on keeping faith with those who served; they wrote no unlimited federal bureaucracy into the document, but they did expect Congress to fulfill specific promises made to the men who won independence. VA’s move stays inside that enumerated power to care for those who bore the battle.
