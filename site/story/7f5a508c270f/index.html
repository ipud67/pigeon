Appeals court denies Trump administration’s mail ballot restrictions

The U.S. Postal Service put a new rule in place in 2024 that set deadlines for mail-in ballots to reach election offices and required local postmasters to collect and deliver them on a fixed schedule. A federal judge in Massachusetts blocked parts of that rule on Friday after lawsuits argued it would keep valid ballots from being counted in the November election.

The Trump administration filed an emergency appeal with the Supreme Court on Sunday morning. Solicitor General D. John Sauer asked the justices to lift the judge's order so the Postal Service could put the full rule back into effect while the case moves forward.

The case now sits before the justices, who must decide whether to stay the Massachusetts ruling or let it remain in force until the lower courts finish their review.

Constitutional analysis
The Constitution leaves election rules to the states. Article I, Section 4 gives Congress power to set the times, places, and manner of federal elections, but the founders expected states to run their own polls and guard the vote. Federalist 59 explains that this arrangement protects liberty by keeping the power close to the people who cast the ballots. California and 22 other states plus the District of Columbia asked the Supreme Court to keep a Boston federal judge’s order that blocks parts of a U.S. Postal Service rule on mail-in ballots. The states argue the rule amounts to an unlawful federal experiment with the franchise. The founders built the government on enumerated powers and reserved the rest to the states under the Tenth Amendment. Early statutes such as the 1792 Militia Act and the Tariff Act of 1789 show Congress acted within clear bounds while leaving day-to-day administration to the states. The right to vote belongs to citizens, yet the states bear the first duty to secure honest elections so that consent of the governed remains real. The Supreme Court will decide whether the Postal Service rule exceeds federal authority or whether the judge’s order stands.
Thinking ahead
The Trump administration filed an appeal with the Supreme Court on Sunday to block a federal judge’s order from Friday in Massachusetts that stops parts of a U.S. Postal Service rule on mail-in ballots. This marks the latest step in the back-and-forth over the rule after the district court ruling. The justices now face a request to pause that order while the appeal moves forward. How long it runs depends on how fast the court acts on the emergency application. If the justices grant the pause the rule goes back into effect until the full appeal plays out or the case ends. If they deny it the judge’s limits stay in place and the government would need to either drop the rule change or win at a higher court level. Next move sits with the Supreme Court. The situation shifts only if the justices issue an order or if the underlying case reaches a final ruling in the lower courts. Horizon: days to weeks on the emergency request.
