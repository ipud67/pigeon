FLORIDA
September 14, 2026 · 16:48 UTC

Immigrants held in small outdoor cages at 'Alligator Alcatraz' in Florida, investigators find

Florida opened a temporary immigration detention center in the Everglades in 2025 under an executive order from Governor Ron DeSantis. The state built the facility in eight days on protected wetland and called it Alligator Alcatraz. The federal government covered operational costs while Florida ran day-to-day operations. Between July 2025 and January 2026 the center held immigrants in housing units that gave them little room to move.

On an unannounced visit in January 2026 inspectors from the Department of Homeland Security's Office of the Inspector General found 79 immigrants had been placed in outdoor metal cages measuring 18 square feet. The cages were used for periods from a few minutes up to nearly two hours, once as discipline. The same inspection turned up showers with bugs, water that detainees said was not clean, plastic cups that could not be washed, showers allowed only three times a week, and medical care and food that fell short of required standards.

The report describing those conditions reached the public on September 14, 2026. By then the state had already closed the center in June after calling it a short-term site. The federal government told the inspector general that Florida held authority over daily operations at the facility.

Constitutional analysis
A federal watchdog report shows Florida ran a temporary immigration detention site in the Everglades where 79 people spent time in small outdoor metal enclosures of 18 square feet each between July 2025 and January 2026. The enclosures, described by operators as calming areas for de-escalation, held detainees for periods from minutes up to nearly two hours and once as discipline. Investigators found the site failed standards on hygiene, medical care, food, drinking water, shower access limited to three times a week in bug-infested stalls, and overall living space. The center opened in 2025 under a state executive order from Governor Ron DeSantis with federal money covering operations, though the state handled day-to-day control. It closed in June 2026 after eight months. The report comes from the Department of Homeland Security Office of the Inspector General, an internal office whose current head was appointed by President Trump in his first term. The Constitution grants Congress power over naturalization in Article I, Section 8, Clause 4 and charges the federal government with securing the border as part of providing for the common defense in the Preamble. The founders viewed controlled immigration and removal of those present unlawfully as necessary to domestic tranquility and the protection of life, liberty, and property for citizens, as reflected in the 1790 Naturalization Act passed by the first Congress. States have historically aided in enforcement under the Tenth Amendment where federal law allows, yet the duty to treat persons in custody with basic order and safety traces to the same founding expectation that government must secure rights rather than abandon them. The report stands as one data point on conditions at a single short-term facility now shuttered.
Thinking ahead
DHS Office of Inspector General inspectors visited the Florida facility in January 2026 and documented outdoor metal cages measuring 18 square feet used to hold 79 immigrants for periods up to two hours. The report lists failures on hygiene, medical care, food, and safety standards, with showers containing bugs and limited to three times a week. Florida ran day-to-day operations under a state executive order while the federal government covered costs; the site closed in June 2026 after being built as a temporary eight-day project in the Everglades. The OIG, an internal DHS watchdog, has no enforcement power so the next observable step is whether ICE or Florida officials issue corrective plans or face follow-up audits. Without a new inspection or lawsuit producing public records the violations stay on paper. This episode likely stays contained to one more round of agency responses and possible state-level reviews. Horizon: 3-6 months until next public filing.
