Taiwan supporters march in New York for U.N. inclusion

Taiwan has sought a seat in the United Nations since it lost that seat to the People's Republic of China in 1971. The Republic of China on Taiwan has never been readmitted under any name. Every year since the early 1990s its government and overseas supporters have held events around the opening of the U.N. General Assembly to press the case for participation.

On September 12 more than 100 Taiwanese Americans, two Taiwanese lawmakers, and civic-group members marched through central Manhattan. They carried signs and chanted for Taiwan's inclusion while the permanent representative of the Marshall Islands to the United Nations, John Silk, walked with them. The route took them past Times Square and Bryant Park and ended near the New York Public Library.

The march occurred on the fourth day of the 81st General Assembly session, which opened September 8. Taiwan's foreign ministry used the same week for additional events in New York to remind member states of its continued exclusion from the world body and its specialized agencies.

Thinking ahead
Taiwan has held this march in New York every year since at least the early 2000s to push for U.N. membership under its own name. Saturday's turnout topped 100 people, included two Taiwanese lawmakers from the DPP, and drew the Marshall Islands' U.N. envoy who repeated public support for Taiwan's participation. The route stayed the same as prior years, ending near the library with the same chants, while Taiwan's foreign ministry ran separate events around the current General Assembly session that opened September 8. The pattern shows no shift in U.N. membership rules or voting thresholds that would let Taiwan in. Next rung is the annual General Debate starting September 22, where a handful of small states may mention Taiwan in speeches but none of the permanent Security Council members have changed their stated positions. The campaign continues at the same scale unless Beijing's diplomatic pressure on Pacific allies eases or a major power flips its recognition policy, neither of which shows up in the current lineup of speakers or resolutions. This stays a yearly visibility event with no procedural opening at the U.N. for the next 12 to 18 months.
