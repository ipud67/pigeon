Pakistan suicide bombing targets police facility in Kohat

Pakistan has faced a rise in attacks in its Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province since the Taliban took power in Afghanistan in 2021. Islamabad accuses the Tehreek-e-Taliban Pakistan of using Afghan territory as a base to plan and launch operations inside Pakistan. Pakistani forces have conducted airstrikes on suspected militant sites across the border, including one in June 2026. Afghan authorities have rejected those strikes and reported civilian deaths from them.

On September 18 2026 a suicide bomber drove an explosives-laden car into a police compound in Kohat. The blast killed at least 16 people, among them five police officers and ten civilians, and wounded more than 30 others according to provincial police chief Zulfikar Hameed. It damaged a mosque where officers, their families and civilians had gathered for worship. Police said two additional militants then tried to enter the compound and were killed in a gunfight with security forces. President Asif Ali Zardari blamed the attack on the TTP.

The site remained under rescue operations on Friday while casualty counts continued to shift. No group has claimed responsibility. The Interior Ministry stated that the assault would not reduce the government's determination to act against such attacks.

The first purpose of government is to secure the right to life so citizens can live in peace, as the Declaration states when it lists life among the unalienable rights and names security of those rights as the reason men form governments. The Preamble lists domestic Tranquility as a core aim the Constitution was written to achieve. When terrorists murder police and civilians at a place of worship, they directly assault that founding purpose. The founders knew internal disorder demanded swift, firm response. Washington himself led militia against the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 to restore order under federal law. Congress passed the Militia Acts of 1792 to give the president power to call out forces against domestic violence. Those measures rested on the plain duty of government to protect life and keep the peace inside the country’s borders. Pakistan’s president pointed to the Pakistani Taliban as the group behind the attack. The Constitution grants no federal power over foreign groups operating from safe havens abroad, yet it does require the government to defend its own territory and people from those who cross borders to kill. The record of the founding generation shows they met such threats with force ordered by law, not with endless debate.

The pattern of TTP strikes on police and security posts in the northwest has run at this pace for four years with no sustained drop. Next steps likely stay inside Pakistan’s current playbook of local sweeps, faster checkpoints in the province and more pressure on Kabul to cut TTP movement. Change would need either a measurable fall in cross-border infiltration or a shift in how Afghan authorities handle TTP logistics on their side of the line. This level of attack frequency looks set to hold for months unless border enforcement or Afghan policy visibly tightens.
