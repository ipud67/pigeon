GREENLAND
September 28, 2026 · 04:00 UTC

Uncovering the Valleys Hidden Below Greenland’s Ice

Greenland sits under an ice sheet that covers 1.7 million square kilometers and reaches more than three kilometers thick at its center. Human eyes have never seen the bedrock directly. Scientists have mapped that surface for decades with radar and other instruments yet many valleys remained undetected or appeared only in broken segments on the existing BedMachine Greenland dataset.

On September 28 2026 a NASA-led team published a paper in Geophysical Research Letters that presents a new map built from Ice Flow Perturbation Analysis. The method reads faint rises and dips on the ice surface captured by satellites then works backward to the bedrock shape that produced them. Researchers traced 1943 subglacial valleys by hand. Roughly one third of those valleys had not been identified before. About half the valleys already shown in BedMachine Greenland now reach hundreds of kilometers farther inland from the ice-sheet edge.

The updated map shows the network begins in southern and eastern highlands where the ice sheet is thought to have first formed and connects into a mountain range near the east coast with relief that grows toward the shore. In the west-central region many valleys run straight and parallel along a southwest-northeast line. Branching angles across the map remain wide. The new layout now supplies a continuous picture of the terrain that earlier maps left fragmented and will feed the next version of the BedMachine Greenland dataset.

Thinking ahead
Scientists built a new map of the rock under Greenland’s ice sheet by tracking faint bumps on the ice surface from satellites and running them through Ice Flow Perturbation Analysis. The map connects 1,943 valleys, adds 650 that earlier maps missed, and shows that half the valleys already in the BedMachine Greenland dataset reach hundreds of kilometers farther inland than recorded before. Many of those valleys start in the southern and eastern highlands where the ice first formed and keep the wide branching angles typical of rivers that cut the land long before the ice arrived. The work sits inside an ongoing effort to update BedMachine Greenland with better bedrock data. Next steps are further satellite passes to check the new valley outlines and plug the sharper map into ice-sheet models that run forward in time. Refinement will stall until the team can match the straight, aligned valleys in the west-central zone against known fault lines or other rock records; without that match the map stays partly unexplained. This mapping effort will run for years as each new satellite dataset and ground check either sharpens or forces changes to the current lines. Horizon: at least through 2029.
