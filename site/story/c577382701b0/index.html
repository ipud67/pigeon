ISRAEL
September 15, 2026 · 07:15 UTC

Strong community ties linked to lower mortality risk, Israeli study finds

Israel's Jewish population has lived for decades inside distinct community models that range from tightly networked rural collectives to dense urban neighborhoods defined by varying degrees of religious observance. Those models set the baseline for a long-term health study that tracked citizens born between 1940 and 1960.

On 15 September 2026 researchers from Jerusalem College of Technology and Ariel University released their analysis of 1.2 million records drawn from the national population registry, education files, health ministry data and tax records. Over a 31-year follow-up the team compared all-cause mortality across four groups and used religious kibbutz residents as the reference. Non-ultra-Orthodox urban residents showed 75 percent higher mortality risk while ultra-Orthodox urban residents showed 27 percent higher risk. Religious and secular kibbutz residents recorded statistically similar rates.

The data now stand as evidence that shared living arrangements, mutual support structures and collective routines reached across the 31-year span and produced measurable differences in survival independent of the religious label attached to each community. The comparison leaves public-health planners with a concrete record of how those arrangements altered mortality patterns inside one national population.

Thinking ahead
Researchers at Jerusalem College of Technology and Ariel University tracked 1.2 million Jewish Israelis born between 1940 and 1960 for 31 years using national registry data. The latest rung shows religious kibbutz residents as the baseline with the lowest mortality risk. Urban non-ultra-Orthodox Jews registered 75 percent higher risk and urban ultra-Orthodox Jews 27 percent higher risk while religious and secular kibbutz groups showed no measurable difference between them. This pattern has held steady across the full follow-up window and is tied to observable differences in daily social contact, shared labor, and mutual aid inside kibbutz boundaries versus urban settings. The gap is unlikely to close soon because the underlying community structures change slowly and the dataset already spans three decades. Next steps hinge on whether public-health agencies test the same cohort design in non-kibbutz rural cooperatives or Arab villages inside Israel; without that data the model stays limited to the four Jewish groups already measured. The study will probably stay in academic journals for the next 12 to 18 months until larger longitudinal cohorts or intervention trials appear. Horizon: late 2027.
