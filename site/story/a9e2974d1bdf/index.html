CJID, NHRC sign MoU to strengthen press freedom and journalists’ safety

The National Human Rights Commission has operated since the National Human Rights Commission Act of 1995 gave it power to protect the rights of everyone inside Nigeria. That law set up the commission as the main government body charged with handling human rights cases, including those that reach journalists. Over the years the commission has chosen its partners with care, only signing formal deals with groups that meet its standards for evidence and follow-through.

On 18 September 2026 the commission signed a of understanding with the Centre for Journalism Innovation and Development at its headquarters in Abuja. The agreement links the commission’s enforcement powers to the centre’s Press Attack Tracker, a database that has logged 1,899 verified attacks on journalists since its launch in 2017 and holds records stretching back to 1986. It sets up joint work on tracking violations, sharing evidence, building skills, and pushing for action on cases the tracker flags.

The memorandum turns the centre’s documentation into material the commission can act on under its mandate. Both sides now share a channel that moves verified attack records directly into the commission’s legal and enforcement processes. The partnership rests on that pipeline and on the commission’s willingness to treat each documented case as a rights matter it must handle.

Thinking ahead
The Centre for Journalism Innovation and Development signed a formal agreement with the National Human Rights Commission in Abuja on 18 September 2026. The deal sets up joint work on tracking press-freedom violations, journalist safety, evidence sharing from CJID’s Press Attack Tracker, capacity building, and advocacy. It builds directly on their earlier cooperation around World Press Freedom Day in May 2026 and follows the commission’s review that CJID met its partnership criteria under the 1995 Act. This kind of memorandum usually leads to a series of working meetings, data handovers, and pilot joint cases within the first six to twelve months. The tracker already holds 1,899 verified incidents dating back to 1986; the next measurable step is how many of those records get routed into the commission’s enforcement pipeline and whether that produces any public complaints or investigations. Change would show up in the annual count of resolved cases tied to the tracker or in new documented attacks that drop because institutions act faster. The partnership runs as long as both sides keep supplying staff and data; without visible case outcomes or renewed funding it tends to fade after the first year. Next likely rung is the first joint review meeting and initial data transfer. Three-to-six-month horizon.
