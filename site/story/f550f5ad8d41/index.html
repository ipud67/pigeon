Supreme Court allows construction on White House ballroom to continue

The National Trust for Historic Preservation sued in federal court in Washington in December 2025. It asked the court to stop above-ground work on a new ballroom at the White House East Wing until the National Capital Planning Commission and Commission of Fine Arts reviewed the plans, the commission approved them, and Congress authorized the project. Senior U.S. District Judge Richard Leon issued an order on March 31, 2026, that blocked above-ground construction but allowed underground work on bomb shelters and bunkers plus any above-ground safety and security measures.

The D.C. Circuit paused Leon’s order before it took effect, then upheld it 2-1 on August 7, 2026. The appeals court gave the government 14 days to ask the Supreme Court for relief or the block would start on August 21. Chief Justice John Roberts issued an stay on that date so the justices could consider the government’s request.

The Supreme Court on August 31, 2026, voted 5-4 to keep construction going while the case continues. The unsigned majority opinion said the Trust likely lacks standing because its member’s claimed aesthetic and historic injury from the building’s scale, height, and mass is not concrete enough for a lawsuit. The majority also found that halting work would cause permanent harm to national-security features tied to the underground installation. Roberts and the three Democratic appointees dissented.

Constitutional analysis
The Constitution vests all legislative powers in Congress, including the power of the purse and the authority to regulate construction on federal property. Article I, Section 8, Clause 1 gives Congress the power to lay and collect taxes and provide for the common defense. Article I, Section 9, Clause 7 states that no money shall be drawn from the Treasury but in consequence of appropriations made by law. The founders designed this system so the executive could not build or alter public structures at will without legislative consent, a check rooted in English constitutional struggles against royal prerogative that the colonists knew well. The right to life comes first. The Trump administration argued that the above-ground ballroom forms an essential part of a secure military complex that protects the president, his family, and staff from threats. Government officials attested that constructing something close to the planned height and mass is crucial to the security of the below-ground installation. Securing the executive against sudden attack serves the Preamble’s aim of providing for the common defense so that government can protect life and maintain domestic tranquility. The Supreme Court on August 31, 2026, granted the administration’s request for a stay by a 5-4 vote. The unsigned majority opinion paused a federal judge’s order that had blocked above-ground work on the White House ballroom. The Court did not rule on the merits but found the National Trust for Historic Preservation likely lacks standing because its member’s claimed aesthetic and historical injury from the building’s scale, height, and massing is not concrete enough under Article III. Chief Justice Roberts and the three Democratic appointees dissented. They argued the construction is likely unlawful without congressional approval and that the majority’s decision was no victory for the separation of powers. The project stood at 65 percent complete as of August 13 and was moving quickly toward total completion. The lower court injunction had allowed underground work on bomb shelters and bunkers to continue along with safety and security measures. The administration cited prior statutes granting presidents authority to make alterations and improvements to the White House and to construct buildings in national parks. The National Trust countered that only Congress can authorize new structures on federal property and that the president must seek both permission and funds. The Court’s stay allows work to proceed while the case returns to the district court.
Thinking ahead
The Supreme Court on August 31 paused a lower court order that would have stopped above-ground work on the White House ballroom. The unsigned majority opinion focused on the National Trust for Historic Preservation likely lacking standing because its member's claimed aesthetic injury from the building's scale and height does not meet the concrete-harm test used in prior cases. This tracks the August 21 administrative stay by Chief Justice Roberts and the August 13 government filing that argued national-security needs for the integrated military complex outweighed the trust's objections. Construction, already described as 65 percent complete on August 13, can keep moving while the district court case continues. The next clear procedural step is further litigation on standing and congressional authorization in Judge Leon's court. Indicators that could shift the track are a final ruling on standing against the trust, or Congress passing explicit funding and approval for the project; without one of those the stay holds and work proceeds. The dispute runs at least until the district court issues its next order, which could take months given the pace of the appeals so far. Horizon estimate: through spring 2027 or until Congress acts.
