The Supreme Court turned away hundreds of cases on Oct. 5, including a school ban on 'Let's Go Brandon' sweatshirts

The Supreme Court has operated for more than two centuries under the power granted by Article III of the Constitution to decide which cases it will hear through a of . Most petitions for review reach the justices during the summer recess and are considered at the long conference held each September before the new term begins. On September 28 the justices met in that private conference to review hundreds of petitions that had piled up.

On October 5 the court released the orders from that conference and declined to hear a First dispute from Michigan over a school district's ban on student clothing bearing the phrase "Let's Go Brandon." The case began in 2022 when two brothers wore the sweatshirts to Tri County Middle School in Howard City, the garments a Christmas gift from their mother. Administrators made them remove the shirts. The brothers sued, claiming a violation of their speech rights. A federal district court dismissed the suit, and the Sixth Circuit upheld that ruling in a divided opinion that treated the phrase as vulgar and therefore subject to restriction.

The justices also turned down petitions on whether federal law bars convicted felons from possessing firearms under the Second Amendment, on challenges to COVID-19 vaccine mandates, and on several other matters. They sent one case back to a lower court for reconsideration under a June ruling that limits judicial review of Temporary Protected Status decisions, and they invited the solicitor general to file briefs on three cases testing whether federal banking statutes override state rules that require interest payments on mortgage escrow accounts held by banks. The denials leave the lower-court rulings in place.

Thinking ahead
The Supreme Court on October 5 released its orders from the September 28 long conference that reviewed petitions piled up over the summer recess. The list denied review in hundreds of cases, among them D.A. ex rel. B.A. v. Tri County Area Schools over a Michigan middle school's ban on “Let’s Go Brandon” sweatshirts, disputes on whether felons retain Second Amendment rights to own guns, and two challenges to COVID-19 vaccine mandates. The court also sent Mullin v. National TPS Alliance back to the appeals court to reconsider in light of its June ruling that bars most judicial review of Temporary Protected Status decisions, and it invited the solicitor general to file briefs on three bank cases about federal preemption of state mortgage-escrow interest rules. These denials keep the lower-court outcomes in place unless parties file new petitions later or Congress changes the underlying statutes. The pattern of denials after each long conference has held steady for decades; nothing in the orders signals a shift in the justices' criteria for granting review. This round of denials will stand for at least the rest of the 2026-27 term. Next move would require fresh petitions on similar facts or legislative changes to the statutes at issue.
