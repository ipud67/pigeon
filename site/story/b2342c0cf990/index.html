CUBA
September 19, 2026 · 10:27 UTC

Cuba suffered a nationwide blackout that left millions without power for 32 hours

Cuba has run its national electricity grid on ageing equipment and imported fuel for years. Fuel deliveries fell sharply after the United States began seizing oil shipments bound for the island at the start of 2026 and after Venezuela cut its daily supply of roughly 35,000 barrels following the detention of its president in January.

On 19 September the entire grid suffered a total disconnection shortly before 2 p.m. local time after a transmission fault combined with poor weather in central provinces. The energy ministry announced the nationwide outage within hours. By late evening operators restored supply to priority circuits that serve hospitals and water stations in Havana and began a gradual restart that continued into Saturday.

The failure is the sixth partial or total blackout recorded this year. Residents in the capital reported runs without power that already stretched past 24 hours, while food, medicine, water and cooking gas remained scarce. Protests have followed earlier outages even though unauthorised demonstrations carry the risk of jail. The grid operator has not set a date for full restoration.

Thinking ahead
Cuba’s power grid went down nationwide on September 19 after a transmission fault hit central parts of the island during bad weather. The energy ministry called it a total disconnection. Restoration started that evening in priority zones around hospitals and water stations in Havana and continued into Saturday. This marks the sixth major outage so far in 2026. The country’s aging plants and fuel imports have been squeezed tighter since the start of the year when the US began seizing oil shipments headed for Cuba and threatened tariffs on any nation that kept sending them. Venezuela used to supply about 35,000 barrels a day until its own flows stopped after the US took Maduro in January. Blackouts now run for 24 hours or more in many neighborhoods, cutting water, cooking gas, hospital equipment, and forcing schools and offices to shut. Residents report the gaps have grown from once a month to nearly constant. The pattern has already triggered small unauthorized protests that carry jail time under Cuban law. Havana keeps blaming the fuel shortage on US sanctions; Washington keeps saying the problem is mismanagement inside Cuba. The next visible steps are continued piecemeal restoration and another cycle of repairs on the same worn equipment. Without new fuel arrivals or major grid upgrades the frequency stays high. The only recorded change that has ever paused the cycle in the past was a temporary lift in oil deliveries. Horizon: months of repeated outages until fuel supply or plant repairs shift.
