Wounds still fresh after 25 years: '9/11 is history we are still in the middle of'

Translated from Dutch · read the original →
The original headlineWonden na 25 jaar nog vers: '9/11 is geschiedenis waar we nog middenin zitten'

The attacks on the World Trade Center and the Pentagon took place on September 11 2001. Four passenger jets were hijacked that morning. Two struck the twin towers in lower Manhattan. One hit the Pentagon. The fourth crashed in a field in Pennsylvania after passengers fought back. Nearly 3 000 people died. Rescue and recovery work at the sites ran for months. Thousands of firefighters police officers and construction workers spent time in the dust and debris.

On the 25th anniversary in 2026 those who lived through the day or worked the pile still carry physical and emotional injuries from the event. Many attend annual ceremonies at the memorial in New York. Others mark it privately. The passage of time has not removed the daily reminders for survivors rescue workers and families of the dead.

The consequences of that single morning continue to shape their lives in concrete ways. Medical monitoring programs track cancers and respiratory diseases linked to the dust. Annual gatherings bring the same faces together to read the names. The event remains an active part of their present rather than a closed chapter.

Thinking ahead
The 9/11 attacks sit 25 years back on the ladder of events that produced today's New York observances by relatives, survivors, and rescue workers who still carry daily physical and emotional effects. Commemorations this year drew the same groups who have marked every prior anniversary with the same mix of public readings, private grief, and calls for continued medical tracking. No new federal policy shift or major court filing tied directly to these wounds appeared in the record this time. The pattern holds steady: annual remembrances plus ongoing VA-style health programs for those exposed to dust and trauma. Change would require either a visible drop in participant numbers or a documented policy cutoff in compensation and monitoring programs. This runs at least another decade on current medical caseloads.
