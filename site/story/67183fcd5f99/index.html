Last Virginia gun ban case put on hold

Overview
(The Center Square) – The last pending state court challenge to Virginia’s new assault weapons restrictions has been put on hold as courts await guidance from the U.S. Supreme Court.
Short history
The Virginia General Assembly passed a 2020 law that bans the sale, transfer, manufacture, and import of certain semiautomatic rifles, pistols, and shotguns defined as assault weapons. It also prohibits magazines that hold more than 10 rounds. Gun-rights groups filed multiple state-court lawsuits claiming the restrictions violate the Virginia Constitution. Lower courts upheld the ban in earlier cases. The final remaining challenge has now been stayed by a Virginia judge until the U.S. Supreme Court rules on a related New York case that could set new standards for firearm . The stay was entered August 24.
Constitutional analysis
The right to keep and bear arms stands near the heart of the founding order because a free people must be able to defend their lives, their families, and their liberty. The Declaration lists life first among the unalienable rights that government is instituted to secure. The Second Amendment, ratified in 1791, declares that right “shall not be infringed,” using the same absolute language the founders applied to religion and speech. Virginia’s new restrictions on commonly owned rifles and magazines directly burden that right, yet state courts have now frozen the last remaining challenge until the U.S. Supreme Court speaks again. The founders knew armed citizens as the surest check on both foreign invasion and domestic tyranny. Federalist 46 shows Madison counting on the body of the people, bearing their private arms, to deter even a standing army. The Militia Acts of 1792 required every able-bodied man to appear with his own musket, bayonet, and ammunition — the very arms the government now seeks to restrict. Virginia’s colonial tradition followed the same rule: every householder kept arms ready, and the state relied on that armed populace rather than a large professional force. Structural limits matter too. The Constitution grants the federal government only enumerated powers; everything else is reserved to the states or the people under the Tenth Amendment. But when a state enacts a blanket ban on arms in common use for lawful purposes, it collides with the Second Amendment’s command, which the founders understood as a limit on all government, not just federal. Putting the case on hold does not settle the question. It simply waits for higher guidance while Virginians’ right to arms remains in limbo. The pause should remind lawmakers that the founding standard puts the protection of life and liberty first, and the people’s arms were the founders’ chosen means to that end.
Predictive model
The last Virginia state-court suit against the 2020 assault-weapons ban just got stayed. Judges are waiting for the U.S. Supreme Court to rule in the pending federal cases that test the same ban under the Second Amendment. That Supreme Court decision is the next clear rung; until it drops, every Virginia case sits frozen. If the high court upholds the ban, these suits likely die. If it strikes the ban down, the state cases restart under the new rule. No other trigger is visible right now. This pause will last until the Supreme Court issues its opinion, expected sometime in the next 9 to 15 months. After that the Virginia courts will either dismiss the suits or reopen them. Horizon: mid-2027 at the earliest.
