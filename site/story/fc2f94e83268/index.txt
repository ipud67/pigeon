18:T516,A federal judge in Washington declined to block the Pentagon from firing three Stars and Stripes employees for alleged insubordination. The workers claimed the terminations violated their First Amendment rights. Judge Trevor McFadden ruled they were unlikely to win that claim.

The First Amendment protects the right of the people to speak and publish without prior restraint by the government. The founders wrote it to guard against the kind of state control over printing they had known under British rule. At the same time the founding generation expected government to run its own operations without internal sabotage.

Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to raise and support armies and to make rules for the government and regulation of the land and naval forces. The Pentagon acts under that authority when it sets standards of conduct for its funded publications. Stars and Stripes has operated for decades as a Defense Department newspaper subject to military oversight, a structure rooted in the same enumerated powers that let Congress create the Navy and the Post Office in 1789.

The judge’s narrow decision turned on the employees’ slim chance of success rather than a broad reading of press freedom.
