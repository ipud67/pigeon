Short history
A federal judge declined to stop the Pentagon from firing three Stars and Stripes staffers on September 5 2026. The employees claimed the terminations for alleged insubordination violated the First Amendment. Judge Trevor McFadden ruled they were not likely to succeed on those claims after a hearing that examined the conduct of two of the workers. Stars and Stripes operates as a Pentagon-funded newspaper that covers the military. The firings followed internal disputes over editorial decisions and orders from Defense Department officials. The case reached the U.S. District Court in Washington where the staffers sought an to keep their jobs.
Constitutional analysis
A federal judge in Washington declined to block the Pentagon from firing three Stars and Stripes employees for alleged insubordination. The workers claimed the terminations violated their First Amendment rights. Judge Trevor McFadden ruled they were unlikely to win that claim. The First Amendment protects the right of the people to speak and publish without prior restraint by the government. The founders wrote it to guard against the kind of state control over printing they had known under British rule. At the same time the founding generation expected government to run its own operations without internal sabotage. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to raise and support armies and to make rules for the government and regulation of the land and naval forces. The Pentagon acts under that authority when it sets standards of conduct for its funded publications. Stars and Stripes has operated for decades as a Defense Department newspaper subject to military oversight, a structure rooted in the same enumerated powers that let Congress create the Navy and the Post Office in 1789. The judge’s narrow decision turned on the employees’ slim chance of success rather than a broad reading of press freedom. The case leaves the firings in place while the underlying lawsuit continues.
Predictive model
A federal judge turned down the request to block the Pentagon from firing three Stars and Stripes staffers over alleged insubordination. The ruling came hours after a hearing that weighed whether the workers had a strong shot at proving a First Amendment violation. The case now moves forward without a preliminary injunction, so the terminations can proceed while the full lawsuit continues in court. The staffers still have the option to appeal the denial or push the underlying claims to trial. A shift would require either a higher court stepping in with a stay or the judge later siding with them on the merits after more evidence. Right now the indicators point to the firings holding unless new filings change the procedural track. This runs at least through the rest of the year as the lawsuit plays out. Next step is likely an appeal or amended complaint.
