North Korean hackers behind crypto thefts across 100 countries, including Japan

North Korean state agencies have run remote IT work and cyber operations for years to bring in foreign currency. The 313 General Bureau of the Munitions Industry Department, which handles weapons development and IT strategy under the Workers Party of Korea Central Committee, has directed both activities. North Korean IT workers living in North Korea, China, and Russia have taken programming jobs under false identities, with supporters in Japan supplying computers, servers, identification documents, and financial accounts to move the earnings back home. Hundreds of millions of yen reached North Korea this way in recent years.

On September 19 the Japanese National Police Agency and six other agencies from four countries released a joint warning document under a public attribution framework. It stated that the North Korean group WaterPlum, operating under the 313 General Bureau, had infected more than 30,000 devices with malware from December 2025 through July 2026. The group posed as corporate headhunters, sent malware-laden files disguised as technical assessments to IT professionals, and stole credentials for around 7,000 cryptocurrency accounts. At least ¥1.7 billion in cryptocurrency moved to accounts the group controlled, much of it taken from the compromised wallets. The IP addresses used in the malware campaign matched those tied to the currency-earning work and job applications.

Japanese police dismantled the support network inside Japan that had supplied the . The shared document links the malware operation directly to the same bureau that oversees the remote-work revenue stream.

Thinking ahead
North Korean group WaterPlum hit more than 30,000 devices from December 2025 through July 2026, stole login details for about 7,000 crypto accounts, and moved roughly ¥1.7 billion in crypto to wallets it controlled. The National Police Agency, FBI, and five other agencies from four countries released a joint public attribution document on September 19 naming the group and tying its malware servers to the same IP addresses used by North Korean IT workers living in North Korea, China, and Russia who took remote jobs under fake names. Japanese police broke up the local support network that supplied computers, servers, ID documents, and bank accounts for those workers. The operation sits inside the 313 General Bureau, the same unit that handles weapons development and overseas IT earnings for the Workers Party. Public attribution documents like this one usually trigger follow-on law enforcement actions such as account freezes, new malware signatures pushed to antivirus firms, and updated takedown requests to hosting providers. The next visible step will be whether other countries issue their own arrest warrants or sanctions packages tied to the named bureau. How long this specific campaign stays quiet depends on whether WaterPlum rebuilds its infrastructure and changes its recruitment lures. The support network in Japan is dismantled for now, so fresh infections would need new overseas helpers and fresh IP ranges. Horizon: months, until either new malware samples surface or another joint attribution drops.
