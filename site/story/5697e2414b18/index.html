A federal judge held the Trump administration in contempt after it deported an seeker to El Salvador twice despite three court orders

The Immigration and Nationality Act gives the executive branch power to order the removal of noncitizens who have final orders of . That power has been limited since at least the early 2000s by federal and court decisions that bar removal to any country where the person is more likely than not to face torture. In January 2025 President Trump directed the Department of Homeland Security to use every lawful means to carry out existing removal orders. Homeland Security then issued guidance allowing officers to send individuals to a third country not named in the original order once they completed notice, fear-of-torture interviews, and any required screening.

On Thursday the Trump administration filed its third emergency application at the Supreme Court in the same dispute. Solicitor General D. John Sauer asked the justices to lift a nationwide injunction issued by U.S. District Judge Brian Murphy of Massachusetts that requires written notice of the specific third country and a fresh chance to contest removal before any such deportation can occur. Sauer told the court the injunction rests on the same legal errors as two earlier rulings that the government has already challenged. He also requested an immediate administrative stay so deportations could resume while the justices consider the petition.

The case now rests with Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, who handles emergency matters from the First Circuit. She ordered the immigrants’ lawyers to file their response by 4 p.m. Monday. The government’s ability to remove criminal aliens to willing third countries without the extra procedural layer remains blocked until the court acts.

Constitutional analysis
Does Trump administration again urges justices to allow deportations to third-party countries secure or erode an unalienable right, and does it rest on the consent of the governed (Declaration; Federalist 10)?
By the founding standardIf the action protects a basic right and rests on the people's consent, it does the one job government is for — "to secure these rights" (Declaration, 1776).
The founding-based tensionIf it strips a right that comes before government, or acts without the people's consent, it flips the founding deal that just power comes "from the consent of the governed."
— Declaration of Independence (1776); Federalist No. 10 (Madison)
Thinking ahead
The Trump administration filed its third request with the Supreme Court on September 24 to lift a district court order that blocks third-country removals. The procedure lets officials send people with final deportation orders to a nation other than the one listed in the order after they check for torture risk. The filing came after a Massachusetts federal judge again ruled the process gave immigrants too little notice and chance to object. This round follows a January 20 executive order directing faster removals of people with standing deportation orders, followed by February and March guidance from Homeland Security on screening and notifying before sending someone to a third country. The same judge issued the first block in March, the appeals court kept limits in place, and the administration returned to the high court twice before. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson ordered the immigrants’ side to file its response by 4 p.m. on September 28. The court can grant an immediate administrative stay that would let removals resume while it weighs the full request, or it can let the lower-court order stand and schedule regular briefing. The dispute stays alive until the justices act on the stay or the underlying petition. Next step is the response deadline, then a possible order within days or weeks. Horizon estimate: weeks.
