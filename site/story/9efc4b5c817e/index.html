WASHINGTON
September 30, 2026 · 14:30 UTC

The Supreme Court heard arguments Monday in Johnson v. United States Congress on when federal district courts may review veterans benefits disputes

The Supreme Court first ruled in 1974 that veterans could bring claims about benefits decisions in federal court. Congress responded 14 years later by passing the Veterans’ Judicial Review Act. That routes every challenge to a benefits decision first through a board inside the Department of Veterans Affairs, then to the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, and finally to the Federal Circuit. The act gives those three forums exclusive power over such disputes and bars district courts from hearing them.

On September 30 the justices will hear Johnson v. United States Congress. Floyd Johnson served in the Army from 1983 to 1985. After Florida convicted him of state felonies in 2013 and sentenced him to 40 years, the VA granted him disability benefits for post-traumatic stress disorder at 70 percent, later raised to 80 percent on appeal. The VA then reduced the rating to 10 percent under a statute that caps payments for veterans incarcerated more than 60 days. Johnson sued Congress in district court in Florida, arguing the cap violates the bill of attainder clause and the Fifth . The district court dismissed the suit in 2022. The 11th Circuit affirmed in August 2025, holding that sovereign immunity blocked the claim against Congress and that the Veterans’ Judicial Review Act made any substitution of the VA futile because district courts lack jurisdiction.

The case now turns on whether the 1988 act allows a district court to hear a constitutional attack on the cap itself or whether every such claim must still travel the exclusive route the statute created.

Thinking ahead
The Supreme Court hears arguments Monday in Johnson v. United States Congress on whether district courts can take constitutional claims against a 1970s-era law that caps VA disability pay for veterans locked up more than 60 days. Floyd Johnson got his PTSD rating cut from 80 percent to 10 percent after his 2013 Florida felony conviction and 40-year sentence. The 11th Circuit ruled last August that the Veterans’ Judicial Review Act routes all such disputes through the VA board, then the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, then the Federal Circuit, and that this setup bars district-court review. This marks the latest step in a line that runs from the 1974 Johnson v. Robison decision through the 1988 act that set the current review channel. The justices will test how far that channel reaches when a veteran claims the cap itself violates the bill of attainder clause and the Fifth Amendment. The case will run at least through the current term. A ruling would set the next procedural fork: either the district court gets the case back or Johnson must restart inside the VA-Federal Circuit track. Observable signals are the oral-argument transcript in October and the opinion release, likely by June. Horizon: June 2027.
