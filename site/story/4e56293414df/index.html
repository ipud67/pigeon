EL SALVADOR
September 10, 2026 · 14:43 UTC

Trump administration keeps TPS in place for El Salvador for now

The United States began granting Temporary Protected Status to Salvadorans in 2001 after a pair of earthquakes struck El Salvador that January. The designation has been extended every 18 months since then by every administration, Democratic and Republican alike, because conditions in the country kept the original disaster-related bar in place. More than 200,000 Salvadoran nationals have lived and worked in the United States under that shield, which blocks and allows them to obtain work permits.

On September 10 the Department of Homeland Security announced that the current TPS designation for El Salvador would remain in effect for now. The decision stands apart from the department’s earlier moves to terminate the same protection for nationals of 13 other countries. No new termination date was set and the agency gave no timeline for a final determination.

The Salvadoran program now continues on its prior schedule while the administration weighs whether to let the designation lapse at the next extension point. The status of those 200,000 holders and the legal machinery that has kept the designation alive for 25 years both rest on whatever decision comes next.

Constitutional analysis
The Constitution gives the federal government power over naturalization and immigration under Article I, Section 8. The founders designed a limited executive that carries out laws passed by Congress, as Washington did when he enforced the Neutrality Act of 1794 and the Alien Acts of 1798. Temporary Protected Status rests on a 1990 statute that lets the executive designate countries for temporary deportation relief when conditions warrant it. The Trump administration's decision to keep TPS for El Salvador in place for now follows that statutory track while ending it for 13 other nations. The founders expected the president to exercise such discretion within the bounds set by Congress, not to invent new categories of permanent presence. Federalist 42 explains that uniform rules on foreigners prevent the states from creating their own inconsistent policies. This step leaves Salvadoran TPS holders under the same legal framework that has governed the program since its creation.
Predictive model
The Trump administration extended TPS for El Salvador on September 10 while it ended the program for 13 other countries. This keeps legal work and deportation protections in place for about 250,000 Salvadorans already here. The decision follows earlier reviews that cut TPS for Nicaragua, Honduras, and others in the first Trump term and again this cycle. DHS has not set a new expiration date or start a fresh review process. The extension holds until officials finish country-condition checks on crime rates, gang activity, and earthquake recovery in El Salvador. A lapse would require the secretary to publish a formal notice ending the designation and giving 60 days notice. The next clear step is a formal DHS decision on whether to terminate or redesignate TPS for El Salvador, expected in the next review cycle. Indicators pointing to change would be a published Federal Register notice citing improved conditions or a court order blocking any termination. This setup will likely run another 12 to 18 months before the next decision point.
