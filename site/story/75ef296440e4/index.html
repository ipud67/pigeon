Fact Sheet: President Donald J. Trump Reinvigorates America’s Hunting Heritage

The federal government has managed more than 640 million acres of public land and 13 percent of the country’s waterways under that set aside large portions for wildlife habitat, recreation and multiple use since the 1960s. Hunting has been permitted on much of that land under rules written by the Interior and Agriculture departments, but layers of federal , seasonal closures and permitting requirements narrowed access over the decades.

On September 17 President Trump signed an executive order that directs those two departments to remove specific regulatory barriers, reopen designated areas previously closed to hunting, simplify permitting for youth and family hunts, and adjust wildlife management plans to support larger game populations on federal property. The order reaches conduct by requiring agency heads to report within 90 days on acreage newly opened and on changes to bag limits and seasons.

The order now sits inside the existing statutory framework that governs federal land use. It turns on which specific closures and rules the departments actually lift and whether those changes remain within the multiple-use mandates Congress wrote.

Constitutional analysis
President Trump signed an executive order on September 17 directing federal agencies to expand public access to lands and waterways managed under Article IV, Section 3, Clause 2 of the Constitution, which grants Congress power to dispose of and make rules for territories and other property belonging to the United States. The order directs agencies to cut regulatory barriers that had limited hunting, to recruit new hunters, and to support healthy game populations on those federal holdings. This action follows the pattern of early federal land policy set by the Land Ordinance of 1785 and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, which opened western lands for settlement and productive use while reserving portions for common benefit. The order also instructs agencies to roll back prior restrictions and restore access without new legislation, exercising the president’s authority to direct the executive branch in its management of federal property. The Constitution places primary control of such public lands with Congress, not permanent agency rules, a boundary tested in the founding era when the first Congress passed the Tariff Act of 1789 and other statutes to fund government through land and commerce rather than standing bureaucracies. A clear statutory amendment or new appropriations rider from Congress would settle any dispute over the permanence of these access changes. The measure aligns with the founders’ view that government should secure the people’s ability to use resources for food, trade, and self-reliance, as expressed in the Preamble’s aim of promoting general welfare through limited federal means.
Thinking ahead
President Trump signed an executive order on September 17 that directs federal agencies to widen public land and waterway access for hunting, cut permitting rules that limited it before, and fund programs to bring in new hunters while keeping game populations stable. The order sits on top of earlier Trump administration moves that opened federal acres and eased wildlife rules in his first term. Agencies now have 180 days to list new hunting zones and report back on rule changes. This runs until the next administration or a court blocks part of it. The order itself sets deadlines for agency plans by early 2027. If those plans face lawsuits over environmental reviews or land-use conflicts, timelines stretch. Without those blocks the changes lock in through at least 2029. Next step is agency lists of opened lands and revised permits. Three-to-five-year run unless blocked.
