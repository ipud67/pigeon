Rebuilding the United States Navy and America’s Shipbuilding Industrial Base
Overview
MEMORANDUM FOR THE SECRETARY OF WAR THE DIRECTOR OF THE OFFICE OF MANAGEMENT AND BUDGET THE ASSISTANT TO THE PRESIDENT FOR NATIONAL SECURITY AFFAIRS SUBJECT: Rebuilding the United States Navy and America’s Shipbuilding Industrial Base By the authority vested in me as President by the Constitution and the laws of the United States of
Short history
In 2026, President Trump signed a memorandum directing the Secretary of Defense, the Director of the Office of Management and Budget, and the Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs to rebuild the U.S. Navy fleet and the domestic shipbuilding industrial base. The order required them to produce specific plans, timelines, and budgets to increase the number of warships constructed each year, expand shipyard capacity, and restore the supply chain for naval vessels and components. It gave the Secretary of Defense lead authority to direct resources and coordinate with private shipyards and steel producers. This action followed years of declining shipyard output, shrinking Navy fleet size from 600 ships in the late 1980s to under 300 by the mid-2020s, and heavy reliance on foreign suppliers for parts. The memorandum set in motion formal executive requirements for annual increases in hull production and industrial infrastructure.
Constitutional analysis
The Constitution puts the common defense first. The Preamble lists “provide for the common defence” right after “establish Justice” as a core reason government exists at all. Article I, Section 8, Clause 13 gives Congress power “to provide and maintain a Navy,” while Clause 1 lets it tax and spend for defense. The founders knew a weak navy invited attack. In 1794 they passed the Naval Act that started building six frigates after Barbary pirates and French privateers seized American ships. Washington signed it and later sent the new frigates to fight the Quasi-War with France. This presidential memorandum ordering the rebuild of the Navy and the shipyards lines up with that founding duty. The executive carries out the laws Congress passes for the fleet, just as Washington and Adams did when they enforced the Militia Acts and the Naval Act. A strong industrial base that can turn out warships in wartime is part of keeping the peace the Preamble calls “domestic Tranquility” by deterring enemies before they strike. The founding generation saw sea power as essential to free trade and national survival; Jefferson and Madison both learned the hard way what happens when the Navy is too small. Rebuilding the fleet and the yards is therefore a return to first principles: government’s first job is to secure the lives and liberty of the people by making sure no foreign power can threaten them.
Predictive model
This Navy rebuild memo drops in August 2026. It orders the Pentagon, OMB, and national security team to start fixing the fleet and the shipyards that build it. The paper trail is still thin—just the memo itself—so the real moves will show up in the next defense budget request, shipyard contract awards, and how fast Congress funds the work. The curve is steepening: 2s10s spread sits at 46 bps, 10-year yield at 4.65 percent, 30-year at 5.19 percent. That tells you borrowing costs for long-term projects are climbing. Shipbuilding is a 20- to 40-year bet, so higher long rates will make big hull programs more expensive on paper and could slow how fast yards ramp up. Watch two concrete indicators next: first new multi-ship contract awards and whether the FY2027 budget adds real money for yard modernization. If those numbers stay flat or get cut, the rebuild stalls. If they jump, the program is moving. Right now the signal is too early to call size or speed. Gap in data. Horizon: 12–24 months before clear contract and budget signals show if it is real steel or just paper.
