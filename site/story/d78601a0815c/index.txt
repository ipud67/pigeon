The measures also extend hours for vote-by-mail drop-off locations. These steps mark an exercise of state authority over the mechanics of elections, a power the Constitution reserves to the states under Article I, Section 4, Clause 1, which lets legislatures prescribe the times, places, and manner of holding elections for senators and representatives, subject only to Congress altering those rules by law.

The founding generation treated election administration as a core state function. In the First Congress, lawmakers passed the Election Act of 1792, which set uniform dates for presidential electors but left ballot handling, voter lists, and polling security to each state’s own statutes and officers. Washington signed the measure without claiming federal power to seize or protect local ballots. The same understanding appears in Federalist 59, where Hamilton explains that state control over elections forms a check against federal consolidation, because the people’s immediate representatives in the states hold the first claim to run the polls.

The right to life stands first among the unalienable rights named in the Declaration, yet here the bills address the machinery that lets free citizens choose their lawmakers and thereby secure that right through ordered government. No clause in the Constitution grants the president or any federal department direct command over state ballot boxes or polling perimeters. The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states or the people all powers not delegated to the United States.

A contested seizure of ballots in Riverside County in 2025 remains before the California Supreme Court, and one of the new felonies would apply to similar future acts by local or federal officers. Federal law still allows the Justice Department to investigate voter fraud, as the First Assistant U.S. Attorney for the Central District of California noted after the signing. 1b:T594,California signed 13 new election laws on September 20 2026. The package makes it a felony to seize ballots, election records or certified voting technology before certification. It also makes it a felony for anyone in authority to order interference with vote-by-mail delivery or return, extends hours for vote-by-mail drop boxes, and bars arrests within 200 feet of polling places on Election Day except for crimes that disrupt voting or harm people or property. These steps follow a state Supreme Court case over a 2025 Riverside County ballot seizure and a federal court block on mail-in voting limits.

The laws sit on top of earlier California rules aimed at the same goal. They target specific actions that could slow or stop ballot counting and voter access. Federal officials have already said the new statutes cannot block lawful probes into voter fraud.

The package will take effect for the November midterms unless a court strikes any part of it down. Next measurable step is whether state or federal agencies test the 200-foot buffer or the felony provisions in the field. If no enforcement actions occur by early voting in October the laws stay on the books with no immediate change.

This runs at least until the midterms conclude and any post-election litigation finishes. It could expand to other states copying the model or to federal suits that narrow the reach.
