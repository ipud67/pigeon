Trump signs law against Russia, threaten China and India

Translated from Dutch · read the original →
The original headlineTrump tekent sanctiewet tegen Rusland, heffingen dreigen voor China en India

The United States has used sanctions against Russia since the country sent troops into Ukraine in February 2022. Those measures blocked certain Russian banks from dollar markets, froze central bank assets held abroad, and barred American companies from most new investment inside Russia. Congress passed and presidents enforced successive packages that expanded the list of blocked Russian officials, state firms, and ships.

On September 19, 2026, President Trump signed a new law that gives the executive branch power to place import tariffs as high as 100 percent on any country that buys above a set volume of Russian crude oil or petroleum products. The also tightens visa and travel bans on additional senior Russian officials and adds new vessel-tracking and insurance rules that raise operating costs for the fleet of tankers used to move Russian oil outside normal channels.

The measure now sits on the statute books and hands the administration a direct lever over the two largest remaining buyers of Russian crude. China and India must either reduce those purchases or face American tariffs on their own exports to the United States.

Constitutional analysis
President Trump signed a law on September 19, 2026, that opens the way for broad U.S. sanctions on Russia, including tariffs as high as 100 percent on nations that buy large volumes of Russian oil and tighter curbs on Russian officials and the shadow tanker fleet. The measure aims to cut the revenue Moscow earns from energy sales to buyers such as China and India. The Constitution grants Congress the power to regulate commerce with foreign nations and to lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts, and excises under Article I, Section 8, Clause 1 and Clause 3. Founders saw tariffs as a core tool of national policy; the Tariff Act of 1789, passed by the First Congress and signed by Washington, set duties to protect American shipping and raise revenue without a standing army or heavy internal taxes. Federalist 41 warned that unchecked foreign influence could undermine domestic tranquility, the very purpose the Preamble lists first for forming the government. This statute follows that tradition by using enumerated commercial powers to deny an adversary funds that could fuel aggression. The law leaves execution to the president, consistent with the Decision of 1789 that placed enforcement of such statutes in the executive branch. No life question arises here, so the structural authority of Congress and the executive under the original design settles the matter.
Thinking ahead
Trump signed the sanctions bill into law on September 19. The measure gives the administration tools to hit countries that buy large volumes of Russian oil with import tariffs as high as 100 percent. It also tightens rules on senior Russian officials and on the tanker fleet that moves Russian crude outside normal shipping channels. This sits on the ladder of earlier US steps that first froze Russian assets, then targeted banks and energy exports, then pressed buyers to cut volumes. The new law adds a direct price on continued purchases by China and India. The next procedural step is for the administration to publish the list of countries and volumes that trigger the tariffs and the list of Russian individuals hit by fresh travel and asset bans. How long the pressure lasts depends on whether those buyers keep taking Russian barrels at current rates or switch suppliers. If China and India reroute purchases or negotiate carve-outs, the sanctions lose bite; if they absorb the tariff cost, the law stays in force until Congress or the next president changes it. Horizon: six to twelve months before the first tariff decisions are tested.
