CNN, MSNBC and Politico sued Trump in Washington over a White House ban on September 21 2026

The White House has long issued credentials that let reporters from chosen outlets enter the building, attend briefings and travel with the president on official trips. That practice dates to the early 20th century when the press corps first organized itself inside the West Wing. Networks and wires also rotate through a pooled camera system that supplies shared video and audio of events too small for every outlet to cover in person.

On Friday President Trump barred CNN, Politico and MS NOW from the premises after he accused them of publishing false stories drawn from unnamed sources. Their reporters found their passes revoked on Saturday. On Monday the three outlets filed suit in US District Court in Washington asking a judge to declare the ban a violation of the First clause that bars Congress from making any law abridging freedom of the press and to order the administration to restore their access at once.

The suit landed the same morning the major television networks withdrew from the pooled coverage rotation for Trump's trip to the United Nations General Assembly in New York. The White House responded by starting its own streaming channel called Trump TV. Trump posted on Truth Social that he would appeal any ruling against the ban and that outlets he called crooked and corrupt should not be allowed inside the building. The case now turns on whether the court treats the credential decision as protected executive discretion or as unconstitutional retaliation based on the content of prior reporting.

Constitutional analysis
CNN, Politico, and MS NOW filed suit Monday in US District Court for the District of Columbia against the Trump White House after their reporters were denied building access Saturday. President Trump announced the ban Friday, citing repeated false stories from the outlets. The networks had already suspended pooled television coverage of the president ahead of his trip to the UN General Assembly in New York. The complaint asks the court for a quick ruling that the ban violates the First Amendment and an order blocking White House aides from enforcing it. Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution gives Congress power to make laws necessary and proper for carrying out the duties of the executive, yet the First Amendment bars Congress from making any law that abridges the freedom of the press. The founders understood press freedom as a check on federal power, not a guarantee of access to every government building or event. George Washington in 1794 called out the Whiskey Rebellion in western Pennsylvania and led militia under the Militia Acts of 1792 to restore order, all while newspapers printed sharp criticism of his administration without losing physical access to federal spaces. The case turns on whether the president’s control of the White House as executive office space falls under Article II, Section 1 executive power or triggers a content-based restriction barred by the First Amendment. A district court injunction would settle the immediate dispute; an appeal would likely follow.
Thinking ahead
CNN, Politico, and MS NOW filed suit Monday in US District Court in Washington after the White House barred their reporters from the building Saturday. The outlets had already pulled out of the shared TV pool that covers presidential events, leaving no replacement crew for Trump's trip to the UN General Assembly. Trump said his team will appeal any ruling against the ban. The case sits at the motion-for-preliminary-injunction stage. A district judge will set a briefing schedule and hearing date in the next few weeks. The First Amendment record shows past administrations lost similar credential fights when exclusion turned on coverage content rather than neutral rules like space or security. How long it runs depends on how fast the judge moves and whether the White House keeps the ban in place while appeals climb. Next rung is either a temporary order restoring access or an administrative stay that holds the status quo until the DC Circuit weighs in. The ladder from Friday's announcement to Monday's filing and pool suspension points to a short procedural fight measured in weeks, not months. One-line horizon estimate: likely back in court within 30 days.
