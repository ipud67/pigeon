18:T59b,A federal appeals court ruled that an El Salvadoran citizen living in Washington, D.C., has no Second Amendment right to keep a pistol because English common law long allowed the government to disarm those who owed loyalty to a foreign power.

The right to bear arms in defense of self and community has roots in the English Bill of Rights of 1689 and the colonial militia tradition that every able-bodied man stood ready to defend his home and his country.

The founders wrote the Second Amendment to secure an individual right that presupposed membership in the political community, as Federalist 46 explained when Madison described the militia as the whole body of the people armed and trained to arms.

English law from the time of the Glorious Revolution treated aliens who owed allegiance elsewhere as outside the full protections extended to subjects, a distinction carried forward in the first American naturalization acts and the Militia Act of 1792, which limited enrollment to free white male citizens.

The court applied that founding-era principle to uphold the federal ban on firearm possession by illegal aliens and certain non-citizens.

This ruling turns on allegiance and membership rather than on any general power to disarm law-abiding residents.

The decision leaves open the separate question of how far Congress may extend such restrictions without violating the text and original understanding of the
