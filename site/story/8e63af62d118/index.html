MONTGOMERY
October 1, 2026 · 16:06 UTC

Justice Department sued Maryland and two counties over mask and id rules for federal officers

The federal government holds authority over its own law enforcement operations under the Supremacy Clause of the U.S. Constitution. That clause has placed federal officers beyond state and local since the founding. Maryland enacted in the public safety code that bar officers from covering their faces during duties and require them to show identification on demand. Prince George’s County and Montgomery County added parallel local ordinances with the same requirements and penalties. Those measures reached federal agents whenever they operated inside the state.

On October 1 the Justice Department filed a complaint in federal court in Montgomery against the state, its attorney general, the police training commission, and the two counties. The suit charges that the mask and identification rules directly regulate federal operations, expose officers to harassment and doxing by forcing them to reveal personal details, and interfere with undercover work and other sensitive tasks. The complaint cites the specific code sections and asks the court to block their enforcement against federal personnel.

The case now turns on whether the Supremacy Clause bars the state and counties from applying those provisions to federal officers. Courts in earlier suits against similar rules in New York, Virginia, Connecticut, New Jersey, California, Philadelphia, and Milwaukee granted preliminary relief to the United States.

Constitutional analysis
The Department of Justice filed a complaint in federal court against the State of Maryland, its attorney general, the Maryland Police Training and Standards Commission, Prince George’s County, and Montgomery County. The suit challenges state statutes and county codes that bar federal officers from wearing masks and require them to show identification in a manner set by Maryland law. Under the Supremacy Clause in Article VI, Clause 2 of the Constitution, federal law stands as the supreme law of the land and state measures may not regulate federal operations. The founders designed a national government with independent authority to execute its laws, as James Madison explained in Federalist 39 when he described the Constitution as partly federal and partly national in its operation. The First Congress passed the Crimes Act of 1790 and other early statutes that protected federal officers from state interference while they carried out duties assigned by Congress. The complaint states that these Maryland rules expose officers to harassment and violence, chill enforcement of federal statutes, and obstruct sensitive operations. The case turns on whether a federal court will enjoin the state and local provisions under the Supremacy Clause.
Thinking ahead
The Justice Department filed a complaint in federal court on October 1 against Maryland, its attorney general, the state police training commission, Prince George’s County, and Montgomery County. The suit targets state statutes and county codes that bar federal officers from wearing masks and require specific forms of identification during operations. This is the latest in a string of similar federal actions against mask and ID rules in New York, Virginia, Connecticut, New Jersey, California, Philadelphia, and Milwaukee, where courts that have ruled granted preliminary relief to the government each time. The case sits at the motion-to-dismiss stage. Federal judges have sided with the government on Supremacy Clause grounds in the prior suits, blocking enforcement of the local rules. Maryland and the counties will file responses, likely arguing their laws protect public trust and officer accountability without directly regulating federal functions. The dispute will continue at least through briefing and a district court decision, which typically lands within six to nine months on these fast-track constitutional claims. It moves next to arguments over whether the state measures stand or get enjoined, then possibly to the Fourth Circuit on appeal. The situation changes only if the government loses at trial level and that loss holds on appeal, or if Maryland repeals the provisions outright. Horizon: six to twelve months to first ruling.
