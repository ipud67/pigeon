US tightens pressure on Cuban nickel with new round of sanctions

Translated from Spanish · read the original →
The original headlineEE UU estrecha el cerco sobre el níquel cubano con una nueva ronda de sanciones

The United States has maintained economic sanctions on Cuba since 1960 under the Trading with the Enemy Act and later that block most trade and financial dealings with the island government. Those measures expanded in the 1990s with laws that penalize foreign firms doing business with certain Cuban state enterprises and tightened further after 2006 when Washington began targeting officials and companies tied to the Cuban military and intelligence services.

On September 17 2026 the Treasury Department added four nickel mining entities four companies linked to military construction projects and three officers of the Cuban Armed Forces to its blacklist of specially nationals. The action bars US persons from any transaction with the listed parties freezes any assets they hold under US and warns foreign banks and firms that dealing with them risks secondary sanctions.

The Cuban government now faces a narrower set of channels for selling nickel a metal that has supplied for decades. The blacklisted entities lose direct access to the US financial system while the officers named lose the ability to move money or travel through US-linked channels. The restrictions sit inside the long-standing framework that turns on which Cuban revenue streams Washington can reach through its banks and allies.

Thinking ahead
The US added four Cuban mining companies, four entities tied to military projects, and three officers from the armed forces to its sanctions list on September 17. This builds on years of similar steps that target nickel output and revenue flows to the Cuban government. The blacklist blocks US persons from dealing with the named parties and pressures third-country buyers to steer clear. How much longer this runs depends on whether Cuba shifts its military or mining ties enough to trigger a review, or whether the US keeps layering new names. Next likely move is enforcement notices against any foreign firms still buying the nickel, followed by periodic updates to the list as new links surface. The situation changes only if Cuba removes the officers or breaks the listed entities apart in a verifiable way that satisfies US review standards. The sanctions stay in place until a policy shift in Washington or a documented restructure in Havana.
