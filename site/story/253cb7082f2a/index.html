SAN DIEGO
September 15, 2026

Twelve Individuals Charged in $10M Home Daycare Fraud Schemes

Federal money has gone to California for decades to pay for childcare for low-income families. The state sends the funds to San Diego County agencies, Child Development Associates and the YMCA. Those agencies approve families for help, then pay licensed home daycare providers directly once the providers send in monthly attendance sheets signed by both provider and parent under penalty of perjury. California rules require a licensed provider to stay on site and supervise the children at all times except for brief qualified absences.

On September 15, 2026, federal prosecutors in San Diego filed complaints charging 12 naturalized citizens and lawful permanent residents from Syria, Somalia, Sudan, Afghanistan and Iraq with fraud. The charges say each defendant got a state home-childcare license, registered with the subsidy programs, then sent in attendance records that claimed they cared for children on days and at times when they did not. The agencies paid out more than $10 million in federal funds based on those records.

Investigators arrested all 12 that morning with more than 250 officers and searched 12 homes listed as the daycare sites. Surveillance video from the homes contradicted the records, including one case where a provider claimed daily care for 23 and 25 children over two months but footage showed children present on only a single day that coincided with a state inspector visit. The cases remain separate complaints.

Constitutional analysis
Federal fraud charges against twelve people in San Diego for stealing more than $10 million in childcare subsidies strike at the core purpose of government: to secure the right to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness by protecting property and enforcing honest dealing. The Declaration of Independence lists life first among the unalienable rights, and the Preamble names “establish Justice” and “insure domestic Tranquility” as reasons men form government at all. The founders knew that without laws against theft and fraud, families could not provide for their children or build stable homes. Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution gives Congress power to tax and spend for the general welfare, but the Federalist Papers, especially No. 41 by Madison, warn that such spending must stay within enumerated limits and never become a tool for plunder. The 1789 Tariff Act and early funding statutes show the first Congress expected strict accountability for every public dollar. The scheme involved licensed home daycare operators who took federal money routed through California and San Diego County programs, then filed false attendance records signed under penalty of perjury. Surveillance showed children present on almost none of the claimed days. This is simple fraud against the public purse, the same wrong the founding generation punished under common-law rules carried over from the colonies. The 1792 Militia Acts and early revenue laws required accurate records and oaths; false swearing brought swift penalties. Washington himself led troops in 1794 to put down the Whiskey Rebellion when citizens refused to pay lawful taxes and attacked collectors. Government exists to stop such abuse so that honest families can keep what they earn and receive the services taxes actually buy. The twelve charged are naturalized citizens and lawful permanent residents from Syria, Somalia, Sudan, Afghanistan, and Iraq. The facts of the case show they obtained state licenses, registered with subsidy administrators, and then submitted records that did not match reality. Federal agents arrested all twelve and searched the listed daycare homes on one coordinated morning in September 2026. The case rests on concrete evidence, not speculation. No broader claims beyond these charges appear in the record.
Thinking ahead
Twelve people got charged in federal court in San Diego on September 15 2026 for filing false attendance records on home daycare licenses. The complaints say each one got California licenses then registered with the county subsidy programs run by Child Development Associates and the YMCA. They turned in monthly sheets claiming they watched dozens of kids on days when surveillance video showed almost no one coming or going except during one state inspection. All twelve were arrested the same morning by a team of 250 officers who also hit twelve homes listed as the daycare sites. The cases are separate but follow the same paperwork trail that pulled more than ten million dollars in federal funds meant for low-income families. The next visible steps are initial court appearances followed by motions to dismiss or plead. Prosecutors will need to turn over the surveillance tapes and the exact attendance sheets to back the perjury counts. The defendants can fight the video evidence or argue the counts do not add up to ten million. Until a judge rules on those motions or a jury decides the facts the charges stay active. This stays in the federal courthouse until pleas or trial dates are set. If enough defendants plead the docket shrinks fast. If they all go to trial the calendar stretches into 2027. Next rung looks like arraignments and discovery fights.
