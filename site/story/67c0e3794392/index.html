Sharpton compares Ocasio-Cortez to Chisholm at voting rights march

“I know people; I was Shirley Chisholm’s youth director,”
Overview
Civil rights leader the Rev. Al Sharpton, one of the organizers of this year’s March on Washington, compared New York Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D) to the late presidential candidate Rep. Shirley Chisholm (D-N.Y.) while introducing her at the event. “I know people; I was Shirley Chisholm’s youth director,” Sharpton stated on stage while introducing Ocasio-Cortez,…
Short history
Rev. Al Sharpton introduced Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez at the August 28, 2026 March on Washington for voting rights. Sharpton told the crowd he had served as youth director for the late Rep. Shirley Chisholm’s 1972 presidential campaign. He then compared Ocasio-Cortez to Chisholm, the first Black woman to seek a major party’s presidential nomination. The march marked the anniversary of the 1963 March on Washington and the 1965 Voting Rights Act, which barred racial discrimination in voting and gave federal oversight of state election rules in covered jurisdictions.
Constitutional analysis
The right to life is the first unalienable right named in the Declaration of Independence, the very reason governments are instituted among men. This weekend’s March on Washington turned into a stage for Rev. Al Sharpton to link Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez with Rep. Shirley Chisholm, two politicians who have backed abortion on demand. The founders never treated the taking of innocent life as a policy choice or a “voting rights” issue; they saw protection of life as the core duty of just government. Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution gives Congress only enumerated powers. Nothing in those powers, the Tenth Amendment, or the founding generation’s acts supports federal marching, federal voting-rights machinery, or federal celebration of leaders who treat the unborn as disposable. Washington himself put down the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 to prove that federal law must be uniform and limited, not a roving license for political spectacle. The Tariff Act of 1789 and the Militia Acts of 1792 show the early Congress sticking to core functions—revenue, defense, order—not identity pageantry. The consent of the governed was meant to secure natural rights, not expand government into every cultural quarrel. When public events in the capital equate champions of abortion with civil-rights pioneers, they invert the founding order: life first, then liberty, then the quiet work of self-government under written limits. This spectacle shows how far we have drifted from the plain constitutional boundaries the framers drew.
Predictive model
Al Sharpton stood on the stage at the August 28 March on Washington and called Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez the closest thing today to Shirley Chisholm, the woman whose 1972 run he once helped organize. The line landed in front of the same crowd that has gathered every five years since the original 1963 march to push voting-rights bills that keep stalling in Congress. Ocasio-Cortez spoke next, repeating her call for federal legislation to restore the Voting Rights Act provisions the Supreme Court gutted in 2013. The comparison is the latest rung in a pattern that stretches back to Chisholm’s own campaigns: veteran civil-rights figures handing the microphone to younger progressive women of color who then use the platform to pressure House Democrats on voting rules. No new bill text dropped today, no floor vote is scheduled, and the procedural next step remains the same one that has sat on the calendar for three years—getting enough moderate Democrats and Senate votes to overcome the filibuster. The story will keep surfacing every time a major anniversary march or primary season brings the same speakers together. It fades when lawmakers leave Washington for recess and rises again when a new court ruling or redistricting fight puts voting mechanics back on cable news. Without a concrete legislative vehicle or an election that changes the House math, the cycle simply repeats. This round runs through the rest of the 2026 midterm cycle. Horizon estimate: fades by late September unless a new court case or bill filing resets the clock.
