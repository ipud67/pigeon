VENTURA
September 18, 2026 · 21:42 UTC
This story is from September 18, 2026 and is no longer on the front page.

Ventura mayor Jeannette Sánchez-Palacios said the southern California city is exploring adoption of the IHRA definition of antisemitism after anti-Jewish vandalism

Ventura has responded to multiple acts of vandalism against Jewish sites and other groups over the past few years by condemning them publicly and stating that the city will not tolerate such conduct. The city already maintains a general policy against hate crimes. On September 18 the mayor of Ventura stood at a press conference at the recently targeted Temple Beth Torah synagogue and said she had directed city staff to examine adoption of the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance working definition of antisemitism.

The definition would give city officials and residents a specific set of examples of what conduct counts as antisemitism. Mayor Jeannette Sánchez-Palacios told reporters the step would build on the existing policy by naming the conduct more precisely. The Temple Beth Torah sign and exterior walls had been marked with a swastika and the phrases “death to the Jews” and “fight k***s.” Nearby Higher Vision Church and Juanamaria Park carried similar markings, according to the Ventura Police Department and the synagogue’s rabbi.

City staff must now complete their review and present findings to the Ventura City Council. The council would then vote on whether to adopt the definition. The mayor said she is still studying the text and noted that San Diego had already passed a resolution using it.

Thinking ahead
Ventura mayor Jeannette Sánchez-Palacios said the city is looking into adopting the IHRA working definition of antisemitism after graffiti reading “death to the Jews” and a swastika appeared on Temple Beth Torah, a nearby church, and a park. Police logged the vandalism as antisemitic. The mayor spoke at an Anti-Defamation League press conference at the synagogue on September 18 and told reporters she has already asked city staff to research the step. San Diego adopted the same definition by council resolution in recent years. Ventura would follow the same path: staff report to the city council, then a council vote. The city already maintains a public stance against hate crimes, with prior incidents including swastikas on political signs. The process is likely to take weeks to a few months, depending on how quickly staff completes the review and how the council schedules it. Next visible step is the staff report landing on the council agenda. If the council votes yes, the definition becomes official city policy; if it tables or rejects the item, the effort stops there. No indicators yet on council leanings or timeline. Horizon: staff report to council within 60–90 days.
