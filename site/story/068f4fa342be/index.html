Declassified CIA document warned Clinton admin in 1998 of possible plane attack

U.S. agencies tracked Osama bin Laden and al-Qaida for years before 2001. The agencies collected reports on plots that included aircraft hijackings and passed some of those reports to the White House in daily briefings that began in the Clinton administration.

On Dec. 4, 1998, one such briefing carried the heading “Bin Ladin Preparing To Hijack US Aircraft and Other Attacks.” It told President Clinton that a source had described a Gama’at al-Islamiyya member who said his group had finished planning an operation in the United States on bin Laden’s behalf, though the plan sat on hold. The same report noted that one leader in the group was preparing to seize a U.S. airliner and that members of bin Laden’s network had received hijack training. Later briefings in December 1998 warned of impending operations that included plans to hijack aircraft before Ramadan. A Senior Executive dated April 9, 2001, under President George W. Bush, recorded that al-Qaida and two Egyptian groups had considered hostage-taking that could involve hijackings. An Aug. 6, 2001, briefing titled “Bin Laden Determined to Strike in US” repeated the 1998 claim that bin Laden wanted to hijack a plane to free prisoners and added that FBI data showed suspicious activity inside the country that matched preparations for hijackings, including surveillance of federal buildings in New York.

CIA Director John Ratcliffe released 71 of these redacted daily presidential briefings on Sept. 11, 2026, the 25th anniversary of the attacks that killed nearly 3,000 people in New York, Washington, and Pennsylvania. The documents now sit in the public record alongside the sequence of warnings that reached two administrations before the four hijacked planes struck on Sept. 11, 2001.

Constitutional analysis
CIA Director John Ratcliffe declassified 71 daily presidential briefings on the 25th anniversary of the Sept. 11 attacks. One December 4, 1998, briefing to President Clinton carried the title “Bin Ladin Preparing To Hijack US Aircraft and Other Attacks.” It reported that al-Qaida and allied groups had completed planning for an operation inside the United States on Bin Laden’s behalf, that a leader planned to hijack a U.S. airliner, and that network members had received hijack training. Later briefings in December 1998, April 2001, and August 2001 repeated the pattern of aircraft-hijack warnings, including surveillance of federal buildings in New York. The attacks on September 11, 2001, killed 2,753 people in New York, 184 at the Pentagon, and 40 in Pennsylvania. The Constitution begins with the Preamble’s promise to “insure domestic Tranquility” and “provide for the common defence.” The founders saw protection of American lives from foreign attack as the first duty of the national government. Federalist 8 warned that weakness invites invasion and that safety must come before every other object of government. Washington himself took the field with the militia in 1794 to put down the Whiskey Rebellion and enforce federal law. The Militia Acts of 1792 placed the able-bodied men under federal call to repel invasion or suppress insurrection. Intelligence that names specific threats to American soil and American aircraft falls squarely inside that core duty. The briefings show the executive branch possessed concrete, repeated notice of planned hijackings years before the attacks. Article II, Section 2 charges the President to “preserve, protect and defend the Constitution” and to act as Commander in Chief. The First Congress passed the Tariff Act of 1789 and created revenue cutters to guard the coast, proving early lawmakers treated border and maritime defense as immediate federal work. When the government receives specific warning of an imminent strike on its people, the founding standard demands action to secure life first.
