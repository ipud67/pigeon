Massie unveils Flock-Off Act aimed at restricting surveillance cameras

Overview
Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) unveiled a bill Thursday aimed at restricting the use of federal funds for automated license plate reader cameras (ALPRs) and biometric surveillance cameras. The Flock-Off Act would block any attempt to purchase, install, maintain, operate or upgrade ALPRs and biometric surveillance cameras for municipalities and local law enforcement. Despite being in…
Short history
Rep. Thomas Massie introduced the Flock-Off Act on September 3, 2026. The bill blocks federal funds from going to any purchase, installation, maintenance, operation or upgrade of automated license plate reader cameras or biometric surveillance cameras used by municipalities or local law enforcement agencies. Massie has opposed federal support for such surveillance tools in prior legislation. The measure stands as the latest effort to limit those specific federal funding streams.
Constitutional analysis
The Constitution starts with the Preamble goal of securing the blessings of liberty to ourselves and our posterity. The Fourth Amendment guards against unreasonable searches by requiring warrants based on probable cause and particular descriptions of places and persons. Massie’s Flock-Off Act bars federal money from buying, installing, maintaining, operating, or upgrading automated license plate readers and biometric cameras used by local police and towns. The founders wrote the Bill of Rights after seeing British general warrants that let agents sweep up information on everyone without naming anyone. Federalist 84 warned that without explicit limits, government would claim power to watch and control the people under the name of general welfare. The Tenth Amendment keeps powers not given to Washington in the hands of the states or the people, so Congress can refuse to fund tools that turn local forces into extensions of a national surveillance net. This bill follows the founding practice of using the purse to check overreach, as the First Congress did when it passed the Tariff Act of 1789 and kept tight control over spending.
