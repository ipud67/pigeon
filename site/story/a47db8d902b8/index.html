German and Russian foreign ministers discussed grain exports and the war in Ukraine

Translated from Dutch · read the original →
The original headlineBuitenlandministers Duitsland en Rusland spraken over graanexport en oorlog Oekraïne

Germany and Russia have not held talks between their foreign ministers since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine began in February 2022. The two governments have traded public accusations over the course of the war, including German claims that Russian agents directed sabotage inside Germany. Last month German authorities reported they found a drone carrying explosives and a detonator on the grounds of a military base in Leipzig and stated that Russia stood behind the operation.

On the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York yesterday, German Foreign Minister Wadephul met his Russian counterpart Sergei Lavrov for the first such conversation. Wadephul told Lavrov that Germany would defend itself against any further such incidents. The two also discussed the continued movement of Ukrainian grain through the Black Sea.

The encounter marks the first direct ministerial contact between the two countries in more than four years. It leaves open whether the two governments will schedule any follow-up talks on either the Leipzig incident or the grain shipments.

Thinking ahead
German Foreign Minister Wadephul met Russian Foreign Minister Lavrov on the sidelines of the UN summit in New York yesterday. They talked about grain exports from Ukraine and the war there. Wadephul also raised the drone with explosives found in Leipzig last month, told Lavrov that Germany holds Russia responsible, and stated that Germany will defend itself. This marks the first direct contact between the two foreign ministers since the Leipzig incident. The conversation sits at the level of routine diplomatic contact on the margins of a multilateral meeting. No joint statement or follow-up mechanism was announced. The next observable step would be whether either side schedules a formal bilateral channel or whether the Leipzig case moves into formal legal or security channels inside Germany or at NATO. Without a shift in the underlying force posture along the Ukraine front or a new incident tied to German soil, the pattern of occasional sideline talks on grain and the war is likely to repeat at future multilateral gatherings. The situation changes only if one government publicly walks away from the format or if Germany escalates the Leipzig case to a formal expulsion or sanctions step. This contact likely continues at the current low-intensity level for months.
