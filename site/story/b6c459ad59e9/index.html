Charge Complaint Procedures

Overview
Rule. The Federal Maritime Commission (FMC or Commission) is issuing this rule to bring awareness that as an alternative to following the Commission's Interim Procedure for Charge Complaints, Charge Complaints may be filed with the Commission following traditional complaint processes involving either an Law Judge or Small Claims Officer. In accordance with the , when a person files a Charge Complaint as a traditional complaint, the Administrative Law Judge or Small Claims Officer must treat the complaint as such, including by requiring the common carrier to bear the burden of establishing the reasonableness of any demurrage or detention charges pursuant to the Commission's Interpretive Rule on Unjust and Unreasonable Practices With Respect to Demurrage and Detention. (Federal Maritime Commission)
Short history
The Federal Maritime Commission set up an Interim Procedure for Charge Complaints in 2020. That procedure let shippers and others challenge demurrage and detention fees directly with the agency. On September 1, 2026, the FMC issued a new rule that adds another option. Charge Complaints can now also be filed as traditional complaints heard by an Administrative Law Judge or a Small Claims Officer. When filed that way, the judge or officer must follow standard complaint rules. This includes placing the burden on the common carrier to prove its demurrage or detention charges are reasonable, as required by the FMC’s existing Interpretive Rule.
Constitutional analysis
The federal government exists first to secure the right to life, liberty, and property so citizens can live and trade in peace, as the Declaration states and the Preamble’s promise of “domestic Tranquility” and “the general Welfare” makes plain. The Federal Maritime Commission’s new rule simply reminds shippers they may file demurrage and detention complaints the old-fashioned way—before an Administrative Law Judge or Small Claims Officer—rather than through the agency’s interim shortcut. That traditional path keeps the burden where the FMC’s own interpretive rule already puts it: on the common carrier to prove its charges are reasonable. This is a narrow exercise of authority over maritime commerce, a field the Constitution expressly grants Congress in Article I, Section 8, Clause 3. The founders regulated shipping tariffs and port practices from the earliest days; the Tariff Act of 1789 and the Decision of 1789 both showed Congress directing how federal officers would handle trade disputes without creating new roving powers. Here the Commission is not inventing new rights or seizing property; it is enforcing an existing interpretive rule that carriers must justify charges that tie up cargo and cost Americans their livelihood. No life is at stake, no unalienable right is threatened, and no enumerated limit is breached. The rule restores a measure of due process and consent of the governed by letting complainants choose the fuller hearing the statute already allows. It aligns with the founding generation’s practical use of federal power to keep commerce moving fairly, exactly as Washington and the First Congress did when they backed the militia and revenue laws that protected honest trade. The FMC has stayed inside its lane.
Predictive model
The FMC just put out a rule that spells out two ways to file a charge complaint over demurrage and detention fees. Shippers can still use the quick interim procedure, or they can go the traditional route with an administrative law judge or small claims officer. In the traditional track the carrier has to prove the charges are reasonable, exactly as the commission’s earlier interpretive rule requires. This setup has been in the works since the 2022 Shipping Act changes. The new language takes effect September 1 2026. After that date, anyone who files through the ALJ or small claims path will trigger the full burden-shift process. The rule itself does not add new requirements or change enforcement priorities. It simply makes the alternative path official so filers and carriers know what to expect. No court action or congressional move is on the table right now. How much longer this continues depends on whether carriers or shippers challenge the rule in court or whether Congress amends the underlying statute. Absent either of those, the dual-track system stays in place. Next observable step is the September 1 2026 effective date, after which the commission will start routing and deciding traditional complaints under the clarified burden rule. Horizon: at least through 2028 unless a lawsuit or new legislation appears.
