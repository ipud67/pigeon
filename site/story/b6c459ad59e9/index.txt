That traditional path keeps the burden where the FMC’s own interpretive rule already puts it: on the common carrier to prove its charges are reasonable.

This is a narrow exercise of authority over maritime commerce, a field the Constitution expressly grants Congress in Article I, Section 8, Clause 3. The founders regulated shipping tariffs and port practices from the earliest days; the Tariff Act of 1789 and the Decision of 1789 both showed Congress directing how federal officers would handle trade disputes without creating new roving powers. Here the Commission is not inventing new rights or seizing property; it is enforcing an existing interpretive rule that carriers must justify charges that tie up cargo and cost Americans their livelihood.

No life is at stake, no unalienable right is threatened, and no enumerated limit is breached. The rule restores a measure of due process and consent of the governed by letting complainants choose the fuller hearing the statute already allows. It aligns with the founding generation’s practical use of federal power to keep commerce moving fairly, exactly as Washington and the First Congress did when they backed the militia and revenue laws that protected honest trade. 1b:T53d,The FMC just put out a rule that spells out two ways to file a charge complaint over demurrage and detention fees. Shippers can still use the quick interim procedure, or they can go the traditional route with an administrative law judge or small claims officer. In the traditional track the carrier has to prove the charges are reasonable, exactly as the commission’s earlier interpretive rule requires.

This setup has been in the works since the 2022 Shipping Act changes. The new language takes effect September 1 2026. After that date, anyone who files through the ALJ or small claims path will trigger the full burden-shift process.

The rule itself does not add new requirements or change enforcement priorities. It simply makes the alternative path official so filers and carriers know what to expect. No court action or congressional move is on the table right now.

How much longer this continues depends on whether carriers or shippers challenge the rule in court or whether Congress amends the underlying statute. Absent either of those, the dual-track system stays in place. Next observable step is the September 1 2026 effective date, after which the commission will start routing and deciding traditional complaints under the clarified burden rule.
