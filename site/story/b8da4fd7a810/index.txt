17:T617,The founders built elections on the consent of the governed, spelled out in Article I, Section 4 of the Constitution that leaves the times, places, and manner of elections to state legislatures, not to unelected boards that rewrite rules on the fly. Changing signature standards for mail ballots hours before those ballots go into the mail undercuts the settled rules voters and candidates relied on, a direct breach of the fixed law the framers demanded so that elections would reflect the deliberate will of the people rather than the convenience of administrators.

The Federalist No. 59 warned that leaving election mechanics to legislatures guarded against sudden shifts that could tilt the outcome. North Carolina’s last-minute guideline on disqualifying mismatched signatures fits the pattern the founders rejected: administrative power exercised too close to the vote, without time for the public or the legislature to weigh it.

This is not how the early republic ran its ballots. Colonial assemblies set clear, stable qualifications and procedures; sudden changes by executive boards would have been seen as the very sort of arbitrary rule the Declaration listed among the reasons for independence.

The right to life stands first among the unalienable rights the Declaration names, yet even after that foundation the framers insisted that legitimate government rests on elections conducted under known, stable law. When officials alter the count standards the night before ballots fly, they weaken the consent that makes every other right secure.18:T436,North Carolina elections officials changed the standards for counting mail ballots on Friday, right before those ballots went out to voters. The new rules add a signature match check that can knock a ballot out if it does not line up. The board vote sets the procedure that local election offices will follow when the envelopes come back.

This change sits on top of earlier steps the board took to tighten mail ballot handling in the current cycle. County boards now have one clear written test to apply instead of earlier looser guidance. Lawsuits over the exact wording have already been filed in past cycles when similar shifts happened close to deadlines.

The situation stays in place until a court blocks it or the legislature passes a statute that overrides the board rule. Without one of those moves the new signature standard will govern the count when ballots start arriving after the September mailing. Next visible step is the first batch of challenged envelopes reaching county canvassers in October.
