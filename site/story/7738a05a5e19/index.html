CUNY says it will not divest from Israel after disclosing investment portfolio sought by anti-Israel activists

New York state universities operate under rules that bar them from selling holdings in companies that refuse to join boycott campaigns against Israel. Those rules trace to Executive Order 157, signed in 2016, which requires state agencies and entities under the governor's control to divest public money from any firm or institution that takes part in such boycott activity.

On September 14, 2026, CUNY released its 2025 investment records after a court settlement with a law student who had filed a Freedom of Information request in March 2024. The records list stakes in Boeing, Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, Palantir, Elbit Systems, General Dynamics, Caterpillar, GEO Group and CoreCivic. CUNY stated it will not divest from any of those holdings and that state law prohibits it from doing so.

The university also confirmed it has rejected every call to join the boycott divestment and campaign. The settlement required CUNY to hand over reports from July 2023 forward, drop its appeal of a 2025 court order, and pay $20,000 in legal fees to the New York Civil Liberties Union.

Constitutional analysis
CUNY will not divest from companies tied to Israel even after it turned over its investment records to activists who filed a public-records lawsuit. The university says New York Executive Order 157, signed by Governor Cuomo in 2016, bars state entities from taking part in boycott, divestment and sanctions campaigns aimed at Israel. That order rests on the settled power of states to direct how their own funds are spent, a power the founders left to the states under the Tenth Amendment when they did not give it to Congress. The records list holdings in defense contractors such as Boeing, Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, and Israel’s Elbit Systems, along with firms like Caterpillar that activists also oppose. CUNY’s refusal follows the plain text of the 2016 order and tracks the founding view that government exists first to secure the lives and property of its people against foreign threats. The First Militia Act of 1792 and Washington’s 1794 call-up of troops to put down the Whiskey Rebellion showed early leaders using public resources and state force to protect domestic order and national defense. No clause in the Constitution gives activists or courts power to force a state university to redirect its endowment against the state’s own policy. The settlement in the records case required only disclosure, not a change in investment rules.
