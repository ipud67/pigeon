ILLINOIS
September 17, 2026 · 21:54 UTC

Muslim doctors sue Illinois over new assisted-suicide law

Four Muslim physicians in Illinois have practiced medicine under state rules that let them decline to perform certain procedures on grounds of conscience. That arrangement stood until this month when the End-of-Life Options for Terminally Ill Patients Act took effect on September 12. The permits Illinois residents who are at least 18, mentally competent and diagnosed with a terminal illness expected to cause death within six months to request self-administered medication to end their lives. It exempts doctors from directly supplying or administering the drugs but requires those involved in end-of-life care to give patients information about the option and, in some cases, to transfer medical records or make referrals.

On September 17 the four doctors filed a federal lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois against state officials. The physicians, represented by the Becket Fund, contend that the information, referral and record-transfer mandates force them to take actions their faith forbids. They say the law leaves them facing professional penalties if they refuse.

The suit asks the court to block enforcement of those specific provisions or to rule them unconstitutional. The case now turns on whether the statute’s requirements for doctors who opt out violate the physicians’ free-exercise rights under the U.S. Constitution.

The Illinois statute forces physicians to facilitate a process they view as contrary to the sacredness of human life. The founders protected religious exercise as essential to ordered liberty. James Madison in Federalist 51 warned that government must control itself so it does not become the instrument of tyranny over conscience. The First Amendment bars laws that prohibit the free exercise of religion, and the founding generation understood that protection to cover acts of conscience as well as belief. The founding standard places the protection of life ahead of any claimed state power to normalize its taking.
