Sumar asks Bolaños to halt of pro-Palestinian activist Fergie Chambers: 'It is political persecution'

Translated from Spanish · read the original →
The original headlineSumar pide a Bolaños frenar la extradición del activista propalestino Fergie Chambers: "Es una persecución política"

Spain and the United States have maintained an extradition treaty since 1970 that lets each country request the transfer of people wanted on criminal charges. Under that treaty the United States asked Spain in July 2026 to hand over James Fergie Chambers, an American activist and heir to a family fortune. Spanish police arrested him that same month while he was on the island of Ibiza. He has remained in a Madrid prison since then while Spanish courts review the request.

On Saturday Enrique Santiago, the parliamentary spokesperson for Izquierda Unida and a deputy from the Sumar , sent a formal letter to Justice Minister Félix Bolaños. The letter asked the minister to suspend the extradition process on the grounds that the case amounts to political persecution tied to Chambers' public support for Palestinian causes.

The request now sits with the Ministry of Justice, which holds the power to stop an extradition before the courts complete their review. Chambers remains in custody in Madrid and the American demand for his transfer continues under the terms of the 1970 treaty.

Constitutional analysis
The United States holds the sovereign right to seek extradition of its own citizens for alleged crimes committed on American soil. James Fergie Chambers, an American, sits in a Madrid jail after his July arrest in Ibiza on a U.S. warrant for providing financial support to designated terrorist groups. The Trump Administration’s request follows standard treaty procedures between Spain and the United States. Enrique Santiago of the Sumar coalition calls the move political persecution and urges Justice Minister Félix Bolaños to block it. The founders placed law and order first among the reasons to form government, as the Preamble states the clear purpose to “insure domestic Tranquility” and “provide for the common defence.” Federalist 8 warned that weakness at home invites both foreign danger and internal disorder. The Jay Treaty of 1794 showed the early republic honoring extradition-like obligations with Britain to keep peace between nations. Article II, Section 2, Clause 2 gives the president power to make treaties with Senate consent, placing foreign requests squarely in the executive lane. Spain may weigh the treaty terms, but the core question remains whether an American who backed terror abroad can find safe harbor by fleeing to Europe.
Thinking ahead

Chambers has been held in Madrid since his July arrest in Ibiza on a US warrant for alleged financial support to pro-Palestinian causes. The next procedural step is the minister’s decision on whether to block the handover under Spanish law, which allows the executive to stop extraditions on public-interest grounds. The request sits inside the standard extradition timeline that runs through Spanish courts and then the cabinet. No court ruling has been issued yet. The situation changes only if Bolaños issues a formal stop order or if the courts complete their review and the government lets the process run. This remains a single-case extradition dispute with no visible shift in bilateral US-Spain treaty mechanics so far. Horizon: weeks to first cabinet signal, months if it reaches full court review.
