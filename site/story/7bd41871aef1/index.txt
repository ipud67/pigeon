1a:T68c,The right to free and fair elections stands at the heart of the American founding. The Declaration lists the consent of the governed as the only just basis for government, and the Constitution in Article I, Section 4 gives state legislatures primary power to prescribe the times, places, and manner of elections for Congress. North Carolina’s decision to open its absentee-ballot window two full weeks earlier than any other state stretches that delegated authority in a way the framers did not contemplate.

The founders feared prolonged voting windows that could invite fraud, intimidation, and the very “corruption and cabals” James Madison warned against in Federalist 10. They designed elections to be public, observable affairs conducted on a single day or short period so that neighbors could watch one another and the process itself could be kept honest. The Militia Acts of 1792 and early state election laws set narrow, fixed days precisely to prevent the slow bleed of ballots that makes chain-of-custody impossible to verify.

North Carolina’s early start does not violate any explicit constitutional clause, yet it runs against the founding generation’s clear preference for tight, transparent election windows that protect the integrity of the vote. States retain wide latitude under Article I, Section 4, but that latitude was never meant to become a race to the longest possible voting season. The people of North Carolina and the Congress that receives its electors still have the duty to insist that every early ballot can be traced, verified, and counted without doubt.
