Supreme Court stayed Fourth Circuit order that told FCC to rule on campaign ad rates in 48 hours

1Cite as: 610 U. S. (2026) Per Curiam SUPREME COURT OF THE UNITED STATES No. 26A476 FEDERAL COMMUNICATIONS COMMISSION v. SHERROD BROWN, ET AL. ON APPLICATION FOR STAY No. 26A477 NATIONAL REPUBLICAN COMMITTEE, ET AL. v. SHERROD BROWN, ET AL. ON APPLICATION FOR STAY [October 14, 2026] PER CURIAM. On March 30, 2026, the Media Bureau of the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) issued a “public notice” about the FCC’s lowest unit charge (LUC) requirements. Those requirements, which are outlined in 47 U. S. C. §315(b)(1)(A), concern favorable rates that broadcasters must charge for campaign advertisements in the weeks leading up to elections. The notice stated that political candidates, joint fundraising committees with noncandidate members, and political parties engaged in coordinated political activity are entitled to the LUC. Four candidates for Congress filed an application for review with the FCC, arguing that only political candidates are entitled to the LUC and asking the full Commission to set aside the public notice as unlawful. Two months later, while their application was still pending before the Commission, the candidates filed a petition for review against 2 FCC v. BROWN Per Curiam the FCC and the United States in the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit seeking the same relief. A divided panel of the Fourth Circuit determined that it had to consider the candidates’ challenge, granted their petition, and set aside the public notice. In doing so, the majority reasoned that the plain text and legislative history of §315(b)(1)(A) limits the availability of the LUC to political candidates. On September 4, 2026, we granted an application to stay the Fourth Circuit’s decision. In our per curiam opinion, we explained that the Communications Act “makes clear” that the Commission must resolve an application for review by order “before an aggrieved party may petition for further review in federal court.” National Republican Congressional Committee v. Brown, 609 U. S., – (2026) (slip op., at 2–3). “Because the candidates’ application for review was pending when they filed their petition for review,” we continued, “the Fourth Circuit likely lacked jurisdiction to address their challenge.” Id., at (slip op., at 3). Indeed, the Fourth Circuit’s holding to the contrary “split[ ] with every other Circuit to have considered the issue.” Ibid. (citing Council Tree Communications, Inc. v. FCC, 503 F. 3d 284, 287 (CA3 2007); Alabama Power Co. v. FCC, 311 F. 3d 1357, 1366 (CA11 2002); International Telecard Assn. v. FCC, 166 F. 3d 387, 388 (CADC 1999) (per curiam)). Just one month later, on October 7, 2026, the same Fourth Circuit panel ordered the Commission to resolve the candidates’ application by October 9, 2026. The Commission thus had 48 hours from the entry of the Fourth Circuit’s mandamus order to issue its final decision. That, the Fourth Circuit believed, was “the only means to finally move forward with. judicial review.” App. to Emergency Application for Stay in No. 26A476 (CA4), ECF Doc. 30, p. 18a, n. 2. The mandamus order came just one week after the Commission’s comment period on the candidates’ 3Cite as: 610 U. S. (2026) Per Curiam application had closed, and in the midst of another comment period for a parallel application the Commission was considering in tandem. The FCC moved before this Court to stay the Fourth Circuit’s mandamus order. On October 8, 2026, the order was administratively stayed so that the Court could resolve the FCC’s application before the 48-hour deadline expired. To obtain a stay, the FCC must demonstrate “(1) a reasonable probability that four Justices will consider the issue sufficiently meritorious to grant certiorari; (2) a fair prospect that a majority of the Court will vote to reverse the judgment below; and (3) a likelihood that irreparable harm will result from the denial of a stay.” Hollingsworth v. Perry, 558 U. S. 183, 190 (2010) (per curiam).
