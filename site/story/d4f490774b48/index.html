President Donald Trump issued a document to reinvigorate America's hunting heritage in Washington

The federal government has managed large areas of public land under that restrict certain uses to protect wildlife and historic features. National monuments created by presidential under the Antiquities Act of 1906 limit activities that could affect the objects they were designated to preserve. Since the 1920s and again in the 2000s and 2010s, proclamations set aside Craters of the Moon in Idaho and Castle Mountains in California with those limits in place. Over the same period Congress and agencies layered rules on hunter education, land access, and that reduced hunting on federal property outside national parks.

On September 17 2026 the president signed an executive order directing the secretary of the interior to open hunting on species not listed under the Endangered Species Act inside both monuments within ninety days. The order states that managed hunting will not endanger the scientific and historic objects the monuments protect. It also requires a general presumption of access for hunting fishing and shooting on other federal lands unless a specific closure is justified by local resource conditions, and it directs agencies to build and maintain roads and trails that improve entry for recreation and wildlife management.

Federal land managers must now review closures and infrastructure plans under the new standard. The order shifts decision-making toward cooperative federalism with states and prioritizes practical public access where it had narrowed. The next required step is the interior secretary’s formal action on the two monuments and the broader policy guidance for all qualifying federal lands.

Constitutional analysis
President Trump issued an executive order on September 17 2026 directing the Secretary of the Interior to open hunting in parts of Castle Mountains National Monument and Craters of the Moon National Monument and Preserve for species not listed under 16 U.S.C. 1533. The order applies a presumption of accessibility to federally managed lands outside national parks, directs expansion of hunting fishing and shooting opportunities, and calls for infrastructure repairs to improve public access. It claims authority under Article II of the Constitution and existing statutes to reverse prior closures and promote cooperative federalism between federal agencies and states. The Constitution grants Congress power under Article IV Section 3 Clause 2 to dispose of and make all needful rules respecting territory or other property belonging to the United States. The Property Clause gave Congress authority to admit new states organize western lands and set terms for their use as the founders expanded the union after the Northwest Ordinance of 1787. Presidents have issued proclamations on federal lands since George Washington signed the first such measures but the order here rests on the claim that the executive may reinterpret monument boundaries and access rules without new legislation. Founding practice treated public lands as a trust held for settlement productive use and the common benefit. The Land Ordinance of 1785 surveyed and sold federal territory to citizens the Migratory Bird Treaty Act of 1918 and earlier state game laws built on the idea that regulated taking sustains populations. Hunters and landowners have long funded wildlife restoration through excise taxes on arms and ammunition first enacted in the Pittman-Robertson Act of 1937 which traces its logic to the founders’ view that citizens who use the land bear responsibility for its care. The order sets a 90-day deadline for the Interior Secretary to implement the new access rules and requires justification only for specific closures based on local resource conditions. A final agency rule listing which lands remain closed or a congressional amendment to the Antiquities Act of 1906 would settle the exact boundary between presidential direction and statutory limits on monument management.
Thinking ahead
The executive order signed on September 17 directs the Interior Secretary to open hunting in two national monuments within 90 days and sets a broader presumption that federal lands stay open for hunting unless a site-specific resource reason closes them. It also calls for new infrastructure to reach locked-up parcels and for streamlined rules on hunter training programs. This is the latest step in a sequence that began with earlier proclamations creating those monuments and later laws that tightened federal land rules. The order itself carries no enforcement deadline beyond the 90-day mark and leaves final closures to agency review of local conditions. Next measurable moves will show up in Interior Department notices listing which parcels actually open and in any legal filings challenging those notices under existing monument statutes. The order will run at least until a future administration revokes it or Congress passes binding new access law. Without either, the presumption-of-access language stays in force and agencies keep issuing the required reviews. Horizon: at least through 2028.
