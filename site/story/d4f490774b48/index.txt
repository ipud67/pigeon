18:T71c,The Constitution grants the federal government only limited, enumerated powers over public lands. Article IV, Section 3, Clause 2 gives Congress authority to dispose of and make needful rules respecting territory or other property belonging to the United States. The founders viewed western lands as a trust to be managed for settlement, not held in permanent federal control. Early statutes such as the Land Ordinance of 1785 and Northwest Ordinance of 1787 divided land for sale to citizens and new states rather than for endless national monuments.

This executive order directs the Interior Secretary to open hunting in two national monuments created by prior proclamations and to apply a presumption of access to other federal lands. It rests on the judgment that regulated hunting supports healthy wildlife populations and does not threaten the objects those monuments were meant to protect. The order also calls for infrastructure repairs and improved access where local conditions allow, outside national parks.

The founding generation expected citizens, including hunters and landowners, to act as direct stewards of game and soil. State game laws and local hunting customs supplied the first conservation rules. Federal overreach that blocks access or overrides those traditions runs against the constitutional design of cooperative federalism and the Tenth Amendment’s reservation of non-delegated powers to the states and the people. 

Opening lands to lawful hunting returns policy closer to the practical liberty the founders practiced on the frontier. It treats hunters as partners in wildlife management instead of subjects of regulatory barriers. 1b:T454,The executive order signed on September 17 directs the Interior Secretary to open hunting in two national monuments within 90 days and sets a broader presumption that federal lands stay open for hunting unless a site-specific resource reason closes them. It also calls for new infrastructure to reach locked-up parcels and for streamlined rules on hunter training programs. This is the latest step in a sequence that began with earlier proclamations creating those monuments and later laws that tightened federal land rules. The order itself carries no enforcement deadline beyond the 90-day mark and leaves final closures to agency review of local conditions. Next measurable moves will show up in Interior Department notices listing which parcels actually open and in any legal filings challenging those notices under existing monument statutes.  

The order will run at least until a future administration revokes it or Congress passes binding new access law. Without either, the presumption-of-access language stays in force and agencies keep issuing the required reviews.
