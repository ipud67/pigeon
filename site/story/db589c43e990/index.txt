1a:T76a,The right to life comes first. The U.S. Constitution exists to secure the unalienable rights named in the Declaration, beginning with the right to life that government is instituted to protect. Uganda has now committed former soldier Michael Katungi Mpeirwe to prison so the justice minister can decide whether to send him to America, where he faces charges that could carry the death penalty.

Founders understood extradition as a tool of justice between sovereigns, not a casual favor. The Jay Treaty of 1794, negotiated under Washington and approved by the Senate under Article II, Section 2, included an extradition clause limited to murder and forgery—crimes that directly threaten life and property. Hamilton defended the treaty in Federalist No. 75 as an exercise of the treaty power that still respected the duty of government to punish violence against innocent life.

Article IV, Section 2 of the Constitution requires states to deliver up fugitives from justice, reflecting the same principle that no safe harbor should exist for those who take life unlawfully. The federal government may enter such compacts with foreign nations when they serve the same end: protecting the innocent rather than shielding killers. If Katungi stands accused of murder or similar violence against human life, extradition upholds the first purpose of lawful government.

Structural limits still matter. The U.S. must respect due process under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments once he arrives, and Uganda must follow its own laws. But the core question is whether this man is charged with destroying the very right—life—that both nations’ governments exist to secure. If so, handing him over serves the founding ideal. If the charges do not threaten innocent life, the extradition loses its moral root in the Declaration and the early treaties.
