18:T861,The settlement in Encinitas Union School District restores parents’ authority over the upbringing of their children when public schools push material on gender identity that contradicts the family’s faith. The right of parents to direct the religious and moral education of their sons and daughters stands at the core of the founding tradition. The Declaration lists the right to life first among unalienable rights, but the same document rests government on the consent of the governed and the duty of families to form the next generation in virtue. The First Amendment protects the free exercise of religion against federal interference, and the Tenth Amendment leaves control of local schools to the states and the people. Yet the founders never imagined government schools could compel children to affirm ideas that violate their parents’ conscience.

The 1791 Bill of Rights and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 both treated religion and morality as essential to ordered liberty. Parents, not distant officials, held the primary duty to transmit those habits. In this case a fifth-grade class at La Costa Heights Elementary read My Shadow is Pink and pressed kindergarten buddies to declare a color for their own identity. Parents who objected on religious grounds were denied notice or the chance to remove their children. The district cited a technical distinction between health class and a “buddy” mentoring program to avoid California’s own notice-and-opt-out statute.

The Supreme Court’s 2025 decision in Mahmoud v. Taylor recognized that parents retain a constitutional interest when public schools require participation in lessons that directly burden sincere religious belief. The settlement now gives families in the district the right to opt out of such activities. First Liberty Institute and the National Center for Law & Policy secured the change through litigation after a preliminary injunction.
