Settlement prompts change at CA school district: allows opting out of LGBTQ+ related material

Parents in the Encinitas Union School District objected when a fifth-grade teacher read the book My Shadow is Pink to students in May 2024. The book addressed a boy's gender identity. The students then took part in a buddy mentoring program that required them to read the book with kindergarten partners and ask the younger children what color best represented them. District officials told parents they could not opt their children out of the activity or receive advance notice of similar lessons.

On September 15 2026 the district settled a federal lawsuit brought by First Liberty Institute and the National Center for Law and Policy. The settlement requires the district to let parents and students opt out of any instruction that includes LGBTQ-related material when it conflicts with the family's religious beliefs. The change follows the U.S. Supreme Court's June 2025 decision in Mahmoud v. Taylor which held that parents have a right to notice and the chance to remove their children from such instruction.

The district also agreed to the preliminary the court had already entered in the case. The new policy now governs how the district handles future classroom materials and peer mentoring activities that touch on the same subjects.

Constitutional analysis
The settlement in Encinitas Union School District restores parents’ authority over the upbringing of their children when public schools push material on gender identity that contradicts the family’s faith. The right of parents to direct the religious and moral education of their sons and daughters stands at the core of the founding tradition. The Declaration lists the right to life first among unalienable rights, but the same document rests government on the consent of the governed and the duty of families to form the next generation in virtue. The First Amendment protects the free exercise of religion against federal interference, and the Tenth Amendment leaves control of local schools to the states and the people. Yet the founders never imagined government schools could compel children to affirm ideas that violate their parents’ conscience. The 1791 Bill of Rights and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 both treated religion and morality as essential to ordered liberty. Parents, not distant officials, held the primary duty to transmit those habits. In this case a fifth-grade class at La Costa Heights Elementary read My Shadow is Pink and pressed kindergarten buddies to declare a color for their own identity. Parents who objected on religious grounds were denied notice or the chance to remove their children. The district cited a technical distinction between health class and a “buddy” mentoring program to avoid California’s own notice-and-opt-out statute. The Supreme Court’s 2025 decision in Mahmoud v. Taylor recognized that parents retain a constitutional interest when public schools require participation in lessons that directly burden sincere religious belief. The settlement now gives families in the district the right to opt out of such activities. First Liberty Institute and the National Center for Law & Policy secured the change through litigation after a preliminary injunction. The outcome places every school board on notice that ignoring parental consent on matters of faith and gender formation collides with the founding order that places the family, not the state, as the first educator.
Thinking ahead
The Encinitas Union School District settled a lawsuit from parents who objected to a fifth-grade class reading My Shadow is Pink in a buddy mentoring program back in May 2024. The deal adds an opt-out for any material that clashes with religious beliefs and follows the Supreme Court ruling in Mahmoud v. Taylor from June 2025 that backed such parental rights. District officials had earlier blocked opt-outs by calling the activity peer mentoring instead of health class covered by state notice rules. Other California districts now face the same policy pressure after this settlement. The change sticks unless a future court narrows Mahmoud or the state legislature rewrites the health-education statute to cover mentoring programs. Next rung likely shows districts tweaking curricula or facing copycat suits. Horizon: 12-18 months until next recorded settlement or injunction.
