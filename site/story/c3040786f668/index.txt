The right to life and personal liberty stands first among the unalienable rights the Declaration lists. Government exists to secure those rights so people can live in peace, as the Preamble puts it when it names “domestic Tranquility” and the “Blessings of Liberty.” The founders knew from bitter experience that a foreign power’s secret agents inside the country threaten exactly that peace. George Washington himself took the field in the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794 to enforce federal law and put down armed defiance that could have invited foreign meddling. The First Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts in 1798 precisely to guard against foreign influence and espionage during a time of real danger from France.

This case does not test American constitutional limits. It is a foreign government punishing an American under its own rules. The Constitution gives Congress power to define and punish “Offences against the Law of Nations,” Article I, Section 8, Clause 10, and the founders wrote the Treason Clause, Article III, Section 3, to make clear that betraying the country to a foreign power is a grave crime. The founders would have seen no tension here with limited government or separation of powers. A sovereign nation has the plain duty to protect its own people from agents of a rival power.

The sentence itself is a fact handed down in Beijing. What matters is that the United States must stay alert to the same threat the founders faced: foreign agents working quietly to undermine the republic that exists first to secure life, liberty, and
