18:T6e9,The United States is negotiating possible military access to a new port on Penrhyn Island in the Cook Islands. The project upgrades the harbor at Omoka near a World War II airfield built by American troops. Washington will pay about $50 million of the NZ$101 million cost, with New Zealand adding NZ$17.5 million. The Cook Islands, New Zealand, and the United States announced the work on the edges of the Pacific Islands Forum in Palau.

The founders placed national defense first among the reasons for forming the Union. The Preamble lists “provide for the common defence” ahead of domestic Tranquility and general Welfare. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to raise armies, maintain a navy, and “provide for calling forth the Militia to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections and repel Invasions.” Federalist 41 explains that safety against foreign danger is the first object of government and that the means must match the scale of the threat.

Early statutes followed the same logic. The Naval Act of 1794 authorized six frigates to protect American commerce. The Militia Acts of 1792 required every able-bodied man to keep arms and stand ready. Washington himself led militia against the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 to show that the federal government could enforce law across distance. These steps treated distant threats and supply needs as proper federal business.

A friendly port that lets Coast Guard cutters and allied vessels refuel and repair fits that tradition of limited, sea-focused power projection. It extends reach without a permanent base or large standing army on foreign soil. 1b:T54c,The US ambassador told RNZ Pacific this week that talks continue on whether American forces or Coast Guard cutters will use the upgraded port at Penrhyn once the NZ$101 million project finishes. The deal was announced earlier this month at the Pacific Islands Forum by the Cook Islands prime minister, New Zealand's foreign minister, and the US deputy secretary of state, with the US putting up about US$50 million and New Zealand NZ$17.5 million. The port sits next to an old World War II airfield, and officials have described it as a shared facility for maritime security rather than a permanent base.

How long this runs depends on when construction wraps and on the separate security talks the ambassador said remain open if the Cook Islands asks for them. The next clear steps are detailed negotiations between the three governments on access rules, vessel sizes that can dock, and what counts as non-military support. Local environmental pushback on any link to seabed minerals could slow those talks or force public adjustments to the stated goals.

Without firm dates for either the build-out or the security side deal, the timeline stays loose. The situation shifts only if one side walks away from the funding split or if the Cook Islands declines any security arrangement.
