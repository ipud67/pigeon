Supreme Court let Trump administration use a modified voter database in Washington on Friday

The federal government has maintained the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements database since the 1980s to let states and localities check whether applicants for public benefits are noncitizens. In late March 2026 President Trump directed the Department of Homeland Security and the Social Security Administration to expand that database so election officials could also verify voters’ citizenship and immigration status. The agencies added records of U.S.-born citizens, granted access to Social Security numbers, and enabled multiple searches at once.

On June 22 U.S. District Judge Sparkle Sooknanan in Washington ruled that those changes violated the Social Security Act, federal privacy , and administrative-procedure requirements. She barred the government from letting states or localities use the modified database. A divided D.C. Circuit panel declined to pause her order on Sept. 4 while the appeal proceeded.

The Supreme Court on Sept. 25 stayed the district-court in an unsigned seven-page opinion. The majority said the lower-court order inhibited the federal government’s assistance to state and local election agencies. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, joined by Justices Sonia Sotomayor and Elena Kagan, dissented. The case now returns to the D.C. Circuit for full briefing on the merits.

Thinking ahead
The Supreme Court on September 25 paused a federal judge’s order that had blocked the modified SAVE database from use in citizenship checks for voter rolls. The unsigned majority opinion cited interference with federal help to states for the November 3 midterm election. The database now holds U.S.-born citizen records alongside immigration data, allows Social Security number lookups, and supports bulk searches after changes ordered in March. The dispute started with an executive order directing Homeland Security and Social Security to expand access for state election offices. A district judge ruled in June that the setup broke privacy statutes and the Social Security Act, noting states had removed citizens based on faulty matches. The D.C. Circuit refused a stay in early September but sped up the appeal. This stay lets the administration keep the system running through the election window. The full appeal returns to the D.C. Circuit for briefing that was postponed pending the Supreme Court’s move. A final ruling there, or a later Supreme Court decision on the statutes, would set the permanent boundary. The pattern of emergency stays this close to voting deadlines points to continued use at least until late October at the earliest. Next step is the appeals court schedule, which could produce a merits decision before or after Election Day. Any shift would require either a circuit reversal or full Supreme Court review that interprets the privacy provisions. Likely to run through the election; next move is D.C. Circuit briefing.
