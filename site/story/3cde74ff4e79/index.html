National Environmental Policy Act Regulations

The National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 required federal agencies to prepare environmental impact statements for major actions. In 2020 the Council on Environmental Quality issued new implementing regulations that narrowed the scope of reviews. Congress repealed those CEQ regulations in the Responsibility Act of 2023 and passed the BUILDER Act, which set new deadlines, limited the range of alternatives agencies must study, and capped the length of environmental documents. The 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act added further rules on reviews and lead-agency authority. The Supreme Court’s 2025 decision in Seven County Infrastructure clarified that agencies do not need to study effects beyond the project’s immediate geographic and footprint.

On July 3, 2025 the Federal Highway Administration, Federal Railroad Administration, and Federal Transit Administration published an interim final rule that updated their own NEPA procedures to match the new statute and court ruling. The rule gave the public 30 days to comment. On September 1, 2026 the three agencies issued the final rule, adopted the interim changes with only minor technical fixes, and responded to the comments received.

Thinking ahead
The final rule locks in changes to how FHWA, FRA, and FTA handle NEPA reviews. It builds directly on the 2023 BUILDER Act, the 2021 IIJA tweaks, the CEQ rule removal, and the Seven County Supreme Court ruling. Agencies took public comments on the July 2025 interim version and made only minor technical fixes before publishing this as final on September 1, 2026. This setup is now locked in unless Congress changes the underlying statutes or a new court case overturns it. No immediate next procedural step is scheduled. The rule stands until someone files suit that reaches a federal court with jurisdiction or Congress passes new legislation. It will likely stay in force for years. Change would require either fresh legislation or a successful legal challenge that produces a conflicting court order. Horizon: at least 3-5 years barring new legislation or major court reversal.
