18:T7a0,The right to life comes first, and the founders saw government’s core job as securing the conditions for people to live in peace and provide for their families. Hunting on federal lands has always been part of that tradition. The Northwest Ordinance of 1787 and early state constitutions treated the taking of game as a natural right tied to self-reliance and the duty to feed one’s household. Washington himself hunted and expected citizens to do the same on public lands.

This Fish and Wildlife Service rule opens or expands hunting and fishing on 111 stations, including first-time access on 14 refuges and 3 hatcheries. It adds more than 1,450 opportunities for ordinary Americans. The agency also cleaned up old rules to cut red tape, make them clearer, and follow plain-language standards ordered by the President. All of it stays inside the property clause of Article IV, Section 3, Clause 2, which lets Congress make rules for federal territories.

The founders rejected unlimited federal power. They expected land the government held to serve the general welfare, not become a permanent bureaucratic estate closed to the people. Early acts like the Land Ordinance of 1785 and the creation of western military reserves treated public land as a resource for settlement, farming, and lawful use by citizens, not a tool for elite control. This rule moves in the right direction by increasing access instead of locking it down.

Federalism still matters. Most wildlife regulation belongs to the states under the Tenth Amendment. The Service should not treat refuges as its own private fiefdom. Yet where the land is clearly federal, opening it to hunters and fishermen honors the founding expectation that public land supports the productive, self-sufficient life of free people.

1b:T4b8,The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service just published the final rule that opens or expands hunting and fishing on 111 stations inside the National Wildlife Refuge System and National Fish Hatchery System. This is the latest step in the ladder that started with Secretary’s Order 3447 and Director’s Order 233, which pushed the agency to cut red tape, use plain language, and widen public access. The rule takes effect September 1, 2026, and adds more than 1,450 separate hunting and fishing opportunities, including first-time access on 14 refuges and 3 hatcheries.

This change is locked in once it hits the books. It will run for the 2026-2027 seasons unless Congress passes a new law, a federal court blocks it on procedural grounds, or a future administration rewrites the orders that drove it. Right now the observable indicators point to steady implementation: the agency has already done the station-by-station reviews, taken public comment, and issued the final document.

No signals of immediate legal pushback or congressional riders have surfaced in the published record. The rule will therefore stay in force at least through the end of the 2027 season.
