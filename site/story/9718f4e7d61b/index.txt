Nine of these members were from Gauteng, with one each from the North West, KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga,” President Ramaphosa said. The annual commemoration is held on the first Sunday of September to remember police officers who died in the line of duty during the preceding 12 months. This year’s ceremony honoured the 12 members, who died between 1 April 2025 and 31 March 2026. For the families gathered at the Union Buildings, the day was about much more than a ceremony. President Ramaphosa said among them was a wife who was pregnant when her husband was killed, while another family lost a father and husband, who left behind infant twins. Among the 12 fallen members was also the only woman on this year’s Roll of Honour – a mother of three from Temba Police Station. The President said these personal stories should force the country to confront the circumstances in which police officers are being killed. “Behind every police killing is a family story that has been permanently rewritten. The circumstances of these deaths should concern us deeply.” Several of the officers were killed while working the same shift, responding to business robbery complaints or transporting awaiting-trial prisoners. Others died while carrying out routine crime prevention and patrol duties in their own precincts. The youngest was just 25 years old. “Today we remember them not as statistics, but as sons and daughters, mothers and fathers, colleagues and friends, who went to work to protect their fellow South Africans and never returned home,” President Ramaphosa said. The 12 members being remembered are Constable Gift Mota, Sergeant Sello Raymond Sema, Sergeant Matome Rapetsoa, Sergeant Simon Masenye, Constable Mwazkhe Khoza, Warrant Officer Mosesi Moswana, Constable Moselalelelo Montja, Constable George Hlekiso, Sergeant Sanele Dlamini, Warrant Officer Vuyisile Sintwa, Constable Sharon Hlangoane and Sergeant Lawrence Mtsweni. Their names have been added to the Roll of Honour, ensuring that their service and sacrifice remain part of the nation’s memory. Collectively building safer communities President Ramaphosa challenged South Africans to look beyond the immediate response to police killings, and consider the kind of society the country wants to build. “We must confront a difficult question: what kind of society kills those entrusted to protect it, and what must we do to change it? When criminals target a police officer, they are attacking the integrity of the State, the rule of law and the 19:T955,, respecting the law, rejecting corruption, reporting criminality and teaching our children that respect for human life is the foundation of a stable democracy.” This, he said, requires investment in families, schools, early childhood development, youth programmes and sport to build resilience before young people become vulnerable to criminal networks. While the number of police officers killed in the line of duty has declined significantly in recent years, the President stressed that even one life lost is one too many. “This reflects the progress of operational interventions, police safety initiatives, community partnerships, and cooperation across government and with the private sector.” Supporting life after tragedy He also called for stronger partnerships with the private sector to support the children left behind by fallen officers. Since its establishment in 2010, the South African Police Service Education Trust (SAPSET) has supported 2 215 children of fallen police officers, with more than R25 million raised through sponsorships. “This investment changes lives. Children, who once faced uncertainty, have graduated. Some have obtained Master’s and PhD degrees,” Ramaphosa said. He appealed directly to corporate South Africa to support SAPSET through sustainable donations and scholarship programmes, helping children transform the experience of loss into opportunity. For the families of the fallen, however, no amount of support can replace the loved ones they lost. “No ceremony can replace a husband or wife. No pension can compensate for the loss of a father or mother, and no tribute can return a parent to their children. “But we can ensure that their service is never forgotten and that their families remain part of the national family,” the President said. To the children of the fallen officers, the President said their parents had left them more than memories. “They left you a legacy of courage, duty and service. Carry their names with pride and build lives worthy of their sacrifice.” To the men and women who continue to wear the SAPS uniform, President Ramaphosa said the nation sees their courage and recognises the demands of their profession.
