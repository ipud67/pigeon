National Park Service v. National Trust for Historic Preservation in the United States

The White House East Wing stood in place for decades until the federal government began a replacement project in October 2025. Demolition of the old structure finished in December 2025. Construction of the new wing, which includes a below-ground military installation and an above-ground ballroom, continued through the first months of 2026.

The National Trust for Historic Preservation sued to stop parts of the work. On March 5, 2026, it asked a federal district court for a preliminary against further above-ground construction. The district court granted that motion on March 31 and later clarified that the order allowed the underground military work to proceed but barred most surface building except what was strictly necessary to protect the White House, the President, and his staff. The court of appeals stayed the injunction on April 17, letting construction move forward while it heard the government's appeal. On August 7 the appeals court upheld the injunction but set its own stay to end on August 21.

The government then asked the Supreme Court to keep the injunction on hold while it prepares a petition for . On August 31 the Court issued the stay. It ruled that the government is likely to show the Trust lacks standing because the single member declaration it offered does not establish a concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent injury in fact under Article III. The injunction therefore remains paused while the case moves forward.

Constitutional analysis
The Supreme Court stayed a lower-court injunction that blocked above-ground construction of a new East Wing at the White House. The National Park Service and other federal agencies had already torn down the old East Wing in 2025 and were building a replacement that includes a below-ground military installation and an above-ground ballroom. The National Trust for Historic Preservation sued to stop the work, claiming harm to its members’ aesthetic, cultural, and historical interests from the finished building. The right to life comes first among the unalienable rights listed in the Declaration of Independence. Government exists to secure that right and the safety of the people, as the Preamble states in its aim of providing for the common defence and securing the Blessings of Liberty. A secure White House with working facilities for the President and his staff serves that core purpose. The Court found the government likely to win on the question whether the Trust even has standing under Article III to bring the suit at all. The founders designed the federal courts for real cases and controversies, not generalized grievances. Article III, Section 2 limits judicial power to those disputes. The Federalist No. 78 explains that courts exercise neither force nor will but only judgment according to the law. Washington himself insisted on energetic execution of federal duties when he led the militia to put down the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 under the Militia Acts of 1792. The government showed it would suffer irreparable harm if forced to halt construction mid-project and that the balance of equities favored letting the work continue. The stay leaves the executive branch free to finish the project while the full appeal moves forward. This matches the founding view that the President must control the executive power under Article II without constant judicial second-guessing of national-security and operational decisions at the seat of government.
Thinking ahead
The Supreme Court just stayed a lower-court injunction that had blocked above-ground work on the new East Wing of the White House. The per curiam order rests on the government's strong chance of showing the National Trust lacks Article III standing, since its lone member's monthly visits and claimed aesthetic harm do not clear the concrete-and-particularized bar set in Lujan and TransUnion. Construction on the ballroom and related features can keep going while the government prepares its cert petition. This phase runs at least until the Court rules on that petition, likely several months from the August 31 filing. The next visible rung is the government's formal petition, due within 90 days, followed by the Trust's response and the justices' decision on whether to hear the case. If the Court grants cert, briefing and argument would stretch into 2027; if it denies, the appeals court ruling stands and the injunction reactivates. The only clear off-ramp is the Trust producing new evidence of standing that meets the Court's test or Congress stepping in with legislation that changes the underlying authority for the project. Without one of those, the stay holds and work continues. Horizon: cert decision by early 2027, construction likely uninterrupted through 2026.
