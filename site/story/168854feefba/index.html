IDF chief Eyal Zamir orders legal review of ‘NAZA’ Gaza war film over ‘blood libels’

The IDF has fought in Gaza since Hamas attacked Israel on 7 October 2023. The military has maintained that its operations in the territory follow international law and target only military objectives.

On 14 September 2026 IDF Chief of Staff Lt-Gen Eyal Zamir ordered the Military General to examine the documentary NAZA and to prepare possible legal steps against its makers. The film, directed by Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor, draws on interviews with 24 soldiers and officers who took part in planning and running systems used in Gaza. It won a special jury prize at the Venice Film Festival on 13 September.

Zamir described the material shown so far as blood , deliberate distortions and false accusations against soldiers and commanders. The same day IDF spokesman Brig-Gen Effie Defrin said the trailer contained harsh and false claims but told military officials to watch the full film before responding. The chief of staff also directed the formation of an inter-agency team to coordinate answers to the .

The order places the film inside the military’s internal legal process rather than leaving it to public debate alone. What happens next turns on what the Advocate General’s review finds in the complete documentary and on whether any charges can be brought under Israeli law.

Thinking ahead
IDF Chief Eyal Zamir ordered the Military Advocate General to review the NAZA film for possible legal steps after its Venice prize and promo clips drew accusations of false claims about soldier conduct in Gaza. This fits the pattern of Israeli officials pushing back on outside accounts of the war that started in late 2023. The spokesperson already flagged the need for full viewing before any deeper reply and called for fact-based discussion. The legal review will take weeks at minimum while an inter-agency team assembles responses to the specific allegations. Next step is the Advocate General’s report on whether the film crosses into defamation or incitement under Israeli law; that could lead to lawsuits against the directors or distributors if the threshold is met. The film itself has not dropped in full yet, so fresh clips or its wide release would force quicker public counters from the IDF. Without a court filing or new leaks from the 24 interviewees, the dispute stays in the review-and-statement phase. If the filmmakers release the complete cut with more named sources, the legal track speeds up. Legal review runs 4 to 8 weeks before any filing decision.
