18:T60f,The executive branch acted through the Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control to remove General François Olenga and the Safari Beach tourist complex in Congo from the list of persons and entities whose assets are blocked under U.S. sanctions. The Constitution gives Congress the power to regulate commerce with foreign nations in Article I, Section 8, Clause 3, and the president the authority to conduct foreign affairs under Article II, Section 2. Congress has long delegated sanctions authority to the executive by statute, a practice that traces to the Neutrality Act of 1794, which let President Washington decide how to enforce non-interference in European wars.

The founding generation expected the president to manage day-to-day relations with foreign powers while Congress set the broad rules. In 1793 Washington issued the Neutrality Proclamation on his own authority, then asked Congress to pass supporting laws. The 1789 Tariff Act similarly gave the executive flexibility to adjust duties for the sake of commerce and peace. This removal of sanctions follows that pattern: the executive decides when a particular foreign person or property no longer threatens the interests Congress has identified in law.

No reasons were published in the Federal Register notice. The decision stands as an ordinary exercise of the discretion Congress has granted the president in foreign commerce and national-security statutes. A future statute or congressional vote could tighten or repeal that delegated power if lawmakers
