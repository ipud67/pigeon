UKRAINE
September 20, 2026 · 09:51 UTC

Trains across Ukraine delayed by up to 14 hours due to Russian attacks

Russian forces have struck rail across Ukraine since the full-scale invasion began in February 2022. Ukrainian Railways, known as Ukrzaliznytsia, has kept most passenger and freight services running by rerouting trains and repairing damage under repeated attacks. On 17 September trains were diverted around a damaged station in Berestyn, Kharkiv Oblast, and around damaged sections of Kyiv's circular commuter line, which produced delays of about six hours on several routes.

On 20 September, Russian strikes again forced Ukrzaliznytsia to halt or slow trains in southern and eastern corridors. The Dnipro-Uzhhorod service reached a delay of 13 hours 40 minutes and the Zaporizhzhia-Przemysl service the same. Routes through Dnipro and Zaporizhzhia recorded the longest hold-ups, while trains serving Kharkiv, Odesa, Kyiv and Cherkasy also fell behind schedule. Commuter runs such as Cherkasy-Uman sat nearly four and a half hours late and Samiilivka-Dnipro four hours late. When air-raid alerts intensify, dispatchers stop trains and direct passengers to shelters or to stay more than 200 meters from tracks and stations.

Ukrzaliznytsia now lists most long-distance and suburban departures as running hours behind their timetables. The railway operator continues to move passengers and freight on the remaining open lines while repair crews work on the newest damage.

Thinking ahead
Russian strikes hit rail lines and stations in southern Ukraine on September 20, forcing Ukrzaliznytsia to halt trains in place for air alerts and reroute others. The Dnipro-Uzhhorod run sits 13 hours 40 minutes behind schedule, the Zaporizhzhia-Przemysl run matches that delay, and shorter routes like Cherkasy-Uman and Samiilivka-Dnipro lag by four to four-and-a-half hours. This follows a September 17 strike that wrecked the Berestyn station in Kharkiv Oblast and parts of Kyiv’s commuter loop, which already pushed some trains onto backup tracks and added six-hour lags. Delays of this size have recurred whenever strikes cut power or damage track in the south and east. The pattern holds as long as Russian forces keep launching missiles and drones at rail chokepoints and Ukrainian crews need hours to inspect and clear lines. Without a drop in strike frequency or faster repair capacity on the southern routes, the same 10-to-14-hour backups will keep showing up on the delay board. Next moves depend on whether Ukrzaliznytsia adds more buffer time to timetables or shifts more traffic to buses for the worst-hit stretches. The situation changes only if strike tempo falls or rail crews get enough warning to keep trains moving between alerts. This level of disruption likely continues for weeks.
