After court loss, Oregon stops blocking Christian widow from adopting over gender identity views

Oregon has run a foster and adoption system that screens applicants on their views about marriage and sex. The state has required those who want to take in children to affirm certain ideas about sexual orientation and gender identity including the use of chosen pronouns and support for medical transition if a placed child requests it. That screening process has operated for years under state rules that treat expressed religious disagreement as evidence an applicant cannot meet a child's needs.

On September 14 2026 the state entered a order and judgment in federal court that ends its block on Jessica Bates. The order requires Oregon to reinstate Bates' adoption application. It bars the state from denying her based on her stated religious beliefs that marriage is between one man and one woman and that a person should identify and live consistent with biological sex. The order also stops Oregon from forcing Bates to use any prescribed words or pronouns about sexual orientation gender identity or gender expression and from categorically disqualifying her for placement over those beliefs.

The state kept the power to weigh an applicant's specific limits including religiously based ones when deciding whether that person can meet the needs of a particular child. Oregon agreed to pay Bates' attorney fees. The order makes permanent an earlier preliminary that had already let her restart the application process after an appeals court ruled she was likely to win on First grounds.

Thinking ahead
Oregon lost an appeals court round more than a year ago on the First Amendment claim. The state then signed a stipulated order on September 14 that reinstates Jessica Bates' adoption application and blocks officials from denying it over her stated beliefs on marriage and biological sex. The order also stops any requirement that she use specific pronouns or language on gender identity and bars categorical disqualification based on those views. The state kept narrow ground to weigh an applicant's limits on meeting a specific child's needs, even if those limits come from religion. Oregon further agreed to pay the prevailing side's legal fees. Similar settlements happened in Vermont this year before its own appeals court issued a ruling. This case now sits closed by consent judgment. Future change would need either a new state policy that tests the order's boundaries and draws fresh litigation or a higher court reversing the underlying appeals precedent. Without one of those observable moves the judgment locks the current terms in place. Likely to stay settled for years unless Oregon tests the placement carve-out in a contested adoption.
