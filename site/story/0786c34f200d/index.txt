1a:T503,The Constitution gives Congress the power under Article III, Section 1 to ordain and establish inferior courts but sets no fixed number for the Supreme Court. The First Congress set the size at six justices in the Judiciary Act of 1789. Congress changed the number six times in the next eighty years, always by statute, never by constitutional amendment. The proposal to lock the Court at nine justices by amendment would have removed that power from future majorities and placed the limit beyond ordinary legislation.

The right to life stands first among the unalienable rights listed in the Declaration. Government exists to secure that right along with liberty and the pursuit of happiness. A Court kept at nine by constitutional text would limit the ability of any single party to expand the bench and shift rulings on life cases that reach the justices under the Fourteenth Amendment.

Founders such as Hamilton in Federalist 78 described the judiciary as the least dangerous branch because it possesses neither force nor will, only judgment. They expected Congress to check the Court through structure and jurisdiction, not through raw addition of seats. The House vote kept open the statutory route the First Congress used and the one followed through the nineteenth
