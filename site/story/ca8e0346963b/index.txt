18:T937,The United States signed a defense agreement with Denmark on September 22, 2026, at the UN General Assembly in New York that permits American forces to establish two new military installations in Greenland at Narsarsuaq and Mestersvig while modernizing the existing Pituffik Space Base. The pact updates the 1951 defense agreement between the two nations, requires mutual consent on all technical details, and explicitly bars any non-NATO state from setting up manned or unmanned military sites or maintaining a persistent military presence on the island. It affirms Denmark’s sovereignty and territorial integrity over Greenland and Greenland’s right to self-determination, with no cession of territory to the United States. The deal must still receive approval from both the Danish and Greenlandic parliaments.

The Constitution grants Congress the power to provide for the common defense, raise and support armies, and maintain a navy under Article I, Section 8, Clauses 1, 12, and 13. The founders viewed a standing army with suspicion and insisted it remain under civilian control and limited by annual appropriations, as James Madison warned in Federalist 41 that unchecked military establishments threatened liberty. Washington himself led the militia to suppress the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 under the Militia Acts of 1792, showing the early republic’s preference for defensive forces tied to clear threats rather than permanent overseas garrisons. The 1789 Tariff Act and the Decision of 1789 further illustrate how the first Congress kept military funding and command tightly linked to legislative consent.

This arrangement rests on a treaty negotiated by the executive and subject to Senate advice and consent under Article II, Section 2, Clause 2. No transfer of sovereign territory occurs, preserving the principle that the United States acquires land only through purchase, treaty, or conquest followed by congressional action, as in the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 and the Louisiana Purchase of 1803. The right to life and domestic tranquility named in the Preamble and the Declaration remain untouched by this pact, which addresses external defense without altering the constitutional limits on federal military power. 1b:T5d1,The US, Denmark, and Greenland signed a defence deal on September 22 at the UN General Assembly in New York. It updates the 1951 agreement and lets the US set up two new bases at the old Narsarsuaq airfield in the south and the remote Mestersvig outpost in the east, while expanding work at the existing Pituffik Space Base in the northwest. The text bars non-Nato countries from putting manned or unmanned military sites or persistent forces on the island, keeps full sovereignty with Denmark, and leaves final details and approval to the Danish and Greenlandic parliaments.

The deal follows more than a year of public friction after Trump called for US control of Greenland on security and resource grounds. Leaders from all three sides described it as mutually beneficial and said construction would start right away once technical terms are locked in.

Parliamentary votes in Copenhagen and Nuuk are the next concrete steps. If both pass, site surveys and engineering work at Narsarsuaq and Mestersvig will set the pace. Any delay in those approvals or disagreement on the still-undefined technical rules would stretch the timeline.

The arrangement holds as long as all three parties keep ratifying the details and no new external pressure forces another renegotiation. Without fresh friction or a parliamentary rejection, the bases move from paper to dirt within the next construction season.
