The Preamble names “domestic Tranquility” and the common defense as core reasons to form government; the Declaration says governments are instituted to protect unalienable rights, beginning with life. Founders gave the federal Congress enumerated powers and left states the rest under the Tenth Amendment, but they never imagined state lawmakers using session-end rushes to pile on regulations that blur lines between protecting life and property and micromanaging daily choices. Washington put down the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 with militia called under the Militia Acts of 1792 to enforce federal law and restore order, showing government’s first job is enforcing known rules that keep people safe, not inventing new ones at the buzzer. The Tariff Act of 1789 and the Decision of 1789 kept power close and accountable so consent of the governed stayed real. These California bills, passed without time for real debate, invert that order: they treat citizens like subjects needing constant state guidance instead of free people whose lives, liberties, and property the law exists to guard. Real founding tradition demands lawmakers respect limits, move slowly, and put protection of innocent life and honest commerce first—not last-minute power grabs that erode the very tranquility the
