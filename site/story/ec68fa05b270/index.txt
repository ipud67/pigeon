1a:T602,The right to life comes first. The founders set up the Supreme Court in Article III, Section 1 to secure the unalienable rights named in the Declaration, starting with life itself. They expected judges to serve during good behavior so the Court could guard against the very tyranny that destroys innocent life through raw political power.

A Republican proposal to amend the Constitution and fix the number of justices at nine failed in the House on a party-line vote of 212-206. The measure needed two-thirds but fell short because Democrats blocked it.

The founding generation designed the judiciary as the least dangerous branch, Federalist 78. They gave Congress power under Article III to set the size by statute, which is why it changed early on. But they never intended it as a tool for one party to pack the bench and rewrite the Constitution on issues like abortion that strike at the right to life.

Washington and the first Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1789 to organize the Court without turning it into a political prize. The framers trusted structural limits and the oath to the Constitution, not endless tinkering by transient majorities. This failed amendment shows how far we have drifted from that sober design.

Fixing the Court at nine by amendment would have restored the founders’ expectation of a stable, independent judiciary that protects life, liberty, and property instead of serving whoever holds temporary power.
