Democrats block proposed to keep Supreme Court justices to nine

In 1937, Congress set the Supreme Court at nine justices by . The number has stood at nine since. On September 2 2026, House Republicans brought a constitutional amendment that would lock the court at nine justices and bar future changes by statute. The measure required two-thirds approval. It failed 212-206 on a party-line vote, with Democrats voting against it.

Constitutional analysis
The right to life comes first. The founders set up the Supreme Court in Article III, Section 1 to secure the unalienable rights named in the Declaration, starting with life itself. They expected judges to serve during good behavior so the Court could guard against the very tyranny that destroys innocent life through raw political power. A Republican proposal to amend the Constitution and fix the number of justices at nine failed in the House on a party-line vote of 212-206. The measure needed two-thirds but fell short because Democrats blocked it. The founding generation designed the judiciary as the least dangerous branch, Federalist 78. They gave Congress power under Article III to set the size by statute, which is why it changed early on. But they never intended it as a tool for one party to pack the bench and rewrite the Constitution on issues like abortion that strike at the right to life. Washington and the first Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1789 to organize the Court without turning it into a political prize. The framers trusted structural limits and the oath to the Constitution, not endless tinkering by transient majorities. This failed amendment shows how far we have drifted from that sober design. Fixing the Court at nine by amendment would have restored the founders’ expectation of a stable, independent judiciary that protects life, liberty, and property instead of serving whoever holds temporary power. The House vote makes clear one party prefers to keep the option of court-packing open.
Thinking ahead
Republicans put up a constitutional amendment to lock the Supreme Court at nine justices. It needed two-thirds of the House to move forward. The vote split straight down party lines, 212-206, and fell short so the measure died on the floor. Democrats have blocked every similar push since the idea resurfaced after the 2020 election cycle. The amendment route is the only way to bind future Congresses on court size; statute alone can be reversed later. Without a two-thirds majority in both chambers and ratification by three-fourths of the states, the number stays open to ordinary legislation. Next move is procedural only: Republicans can reintroduce the measure in the next Congress if they hold or gain seats. Real change would require either a sustained Republican supermajority or a shift in enough Democratic votes to clear the two-thirds bar. Right now the indicators show no such shift. Horizon: Stays blocked at least through 2028 unless seat counts change sharply.
