New Crops, Health Research Proposals to Help NASA Advance Exploration

NASA has run the International Space Station since 1998 as a place to test how people and living things hold up in low gravity and high radiation. The agency has also kept crews aboard for months at a time while it plans longer trips to the Moon and Mars. Those plans have required steady work on closed-loop life support that turns plants and microbes into air, water and food.

On September 14 2026 NASA picked 12 new investigations from proposals submitted under its 2024 research announcement. Six fall under the Space Crops grants and examine how edible plants grow in space-like conditions, how their microbes behave, how resurrection plants survive extreme dryness, and which algal genes change their activity during flight so they can be engineered into life-support systems. The other six come from the Biological and Physical Sciences Division and test what lunar dust does to lungs and immune cells, which existing drugs blunt the effects of space travel, how radiation plus other stressors change organs and tissues that age, and which molecular switches alter gene expression or protein work under those stresses.

The selected teams, nine of them first-time recipients, will share more than 7 million dollars from fiscal years 2026 through 2030. The work sits inside NASA’s larger effort to turn biological systems into reliable tools for crews that must stay healthy and fed far from Earth supply lines.

Thinking ahead
NASA picked 12 new studies on Monday to push forward food production and crew health for long trips away from Earth. The six crop grants build on years of ISS plant tests by looking at how edible plants, microbes, resurrection plants, and algae genes hold up in space or on other surfaces. The six health grants pick up from earlier radiation and dust work by testing lung and immune reactions to lunar dirt, screening existing drugs for protection, and tracking how combined stressors change organs, genes, and proteins. These grants run through fiscal 2030 and mark first-time NASA Space Biology funding for nine of the lead researchers. Next step is the usual award paperwork and lab kickoff at the ten universities spread across eight states. The work changes only if Congress cuts the Biological and Physical Sciences budget or if ROSES-2025 drops a different set of priorities. The studies will likely run their full five-year course with steady reports on results. Horizon: through 2030.
