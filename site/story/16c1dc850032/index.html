Florida attorney general sues Netflix over ‘bait-and-switch’ on privacy for kids

Florida law requires companies that collect personal data from children under 18 to get verifiable parental consent before they do so and to let parents review or delete what was gathered. The rule took effect in 2021 as part of a broader package that gave the attorney general power to sue violators directly in state court.

On September 10 the attorney general filed suit in Florida circuit court charging that Netflix told parents its Kids profile blocked data collection and targeted ads while the service continued to gather viewing history, device information, and location data from those accounts and used the information for its own purposes. The complaint calls the gap between the statements on screen and the actual data practices a bait-and-switch that kept parents from exercising the rights the gave them.

The case now sits in circuit court where the state seeks civil penalties, restitution, and an order requiring Netflix to change how it handles children’s accounts and to stop the challenged statements. Netflix has not yet filed an answer.

The right to domestic tranquility in the Preamble rests on government keeping order so families can raise children in peace without hidden corporate interference. The founders saw protection of the household as the first duty of ordered liberty. The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states powers not given to Congress, including the historic police power to shield minors from fraud and exploitation in the marketplace. Florida acts here in that colonial tradition of local authority over contracts, trade, and the welfare of the young. The suit tests whether a platform that reaches every home must answer in state court when it breaks its own promises about data it gathers on children.

The complaint follows years of state-level scrutiny over how streaming services handle children's viewing data and parental controls. Next step is Netflix filing a response in state court and any early motions to dismiss. The case will likely drag on for 12 to 24 months unless the two sides settle. Indicators pointing that way include the detailed complaint language that sets up discovery on internal Netflix documents and the history of similar state AG suits against tech platforms that often reach negotiated fixes rather than trial. Change would show up if a judge narrows the claims early or if Florida signals it wants only policy tweaks instead of big penalties. If Netflix wins a dismissal on First Amendment or federal preemption grounds the suit ends fast. If the state pushes past summary judgment the fight shifts to data logs and ad-targeting records. Horizon: Likely drags into 2027 or 2028 without early settlement.
