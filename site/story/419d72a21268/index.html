Trump says China 'spies' on US after report ties Chinese imagery to Iran strike

The United States and China have run operations against each other for decades. American presidents have repeatedly accused Beijing of using commercial and government satellites to collect imagery over U.S. military sites, diplomatic facilities and allied bases. Beijing has denied the charges and pointed to documented U.S. reconnaissance flights and satellite passes over Chinese territory and waters.

On September 14, 2026, President Trump stated that China spies on the United States. He made the comment after a new report connected Chinese satellite imagery to targeting data used in Iran's missile strike on American troops in July. Trump added that the U.S. spies on China too.

The exchange leaves the two governments trading public accusations of mutual surveillance while the specific chain between the Chinese imagery and the Iranian strike remains under examination by U.S. officials.

Thinking ahead
Trump called out Chinese satellite imagery in the July Iran missile strike on US troops and said the US spies on China too. The statement follows a report that tied the imagery to the attack. No new sanctions or official probes have been announced yet. This exchange sits on top of years of mutual spying accusations between the two governments. Both sides have kept the public back-and-forth going without it freezing trade talks or military channels. The pattern holds steady unless one side releases fresh evidence or moves to block specific satellite overflights. Right now the indicators stay thin: one presidential remark tied to one report. No visible shift in diplomatic schedules or intelligence-sharing rules. The back-and-forth likely runs another few weeks at the current volume. It moves next only if Beijing answers with its own data dump or Washington adds a concrete restriction on Chinese satellites. Without either trigger the talk stays talk. Horizon: 3-6 weeks of similar statements unless new imagery or a formal protest appears.
