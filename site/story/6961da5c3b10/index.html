US the International Criminal Court to cut off its money

The United States has never joined the International Criminal Court established in 2002 under the Rome to try individuals for genocide war crimes and crimes against humanity. Presidents of both parties have blocked any exercise of its over American citizens or service members. In 2020 during his first term President Trump issued executive orders that froze assets of ICC officials and banned them from entering the United States over probes that could reach US forces in Afghanistan.

On October 9 2026 Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced fresh sanctions that prohibit all financial and commercial transactions with the court itself. The measure cuts off resources the ICC receives from member states and private donors and bars US persons from any dealing that could support its operations.

The court responded that it would keep carrying out its mandate on the basis of evidence and the Rome Statute which now has 124 member countries. A group of European allies that belong to the court said the sanctions will hit its staff and budget while Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu welcomed the step. The US campaign now turns on whether member states continue funding the court or adjust their policies to avoid further measures.

Constitutional analysis
The United States imposed sanctions on the International Criminal Court on October 9 2026 to block transactions with the body and cut off its resources. Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated the move protects Americans from prosecution by the court created under the 2002 Rome Statute treaty which the United States never ratified. The Constitution grants the president broad authority over foreign affairs under Article II Section 2 and gives Congress power to regulate commerce with foreign nations under Article I Section 8 Clause 3. The founders designed these tools so the national government could defend the country without submitting its citizens or soldiers to foreign tribunals lacking the consent of the governed. The Declaration of Independence lists among the king’s abuses that he combined with others to subject Americans to a jurisdiction foreign to their constitution and unacknowledged by their laws. In 1798 Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts and the Logan Act to guard against foreign influence over American citizens and officials. The Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall be held to answer for a capital or otherwise infamous crime unless on a presentment or indictment of a grand jury drawn from the community where the offense occurred. The ICC claims power to investigate actions in Afghanistan that could reach American personnel even though the United States is not a party to the Rome Statute. The sanctions follow the same line as executive actions in 2020 that targeted ICC officials over the same Afghanistan probe. No court ruling or statute has yet settled whether the president may block all funding and dealings with an international body that asserts jurisdiction over non-party nationals.
Thinking ahead
The US announced sanctions on the International Criminal Court on October 9 2026 cutting off transactions and resources as part of a campaign that began in the first Trump term with opposition to probes into actions in Afghanistan. Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated the measures will block any prosecution of Americans and target the court's operations while ICC President Tomoko Akane who already faces US sanctions responded that the court will keep working on cases under the Rome Statute treaty with 125 member states. The move followed the awarding of the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize to former ICC judge Navi Pillay and a statement from the Nobel Committee that referenced judges and institutions under sanction. The sanctions add to existing US non-recognition of the court's jurisdiction which has not stopped it from issuing warrants on referrals from member states against officials from non-members such as Russia and Israel. Next steps depend on whether the court secures alternative funding from its 125 parties or faces further transaction bans that limit staff travel and case work in The Hague. Change would require either a US policy reversal after a future election or successful legal challenges in member-state courts that test the sanctions reach. This round of measures is likely to hold through the current administration with the court continuing limited operations on existing dockets. Horizon: 18-24 months barring a shift in US leadership or funding.
