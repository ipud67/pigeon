1a:T781,The Constitution gives Congress alone the authority to admit new states, dispose of territory, and regulate commerce with foreign nations (Art. I, Sec. 8, Cl. 3; Art. IV, Sec. 3). It says nothing about the president unilaterally renaming a shared international body of water that forms part of the border with Canada. The founders set up a government of enumerated powers precisely to stop one man from treating sovereign neighbors or settled geography as personal property. Washington himself refused to exceed his authority even when he led the militia to put down the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794; he acted under statutes passed by Congress, not by decree.

The order also pokes at the peace the Preamble says government must secure. The founders knew commerce and good relations with neighbors helped keep the domestic tranquility they prized. Hamilton in Federalist 11 warned that weak or arrogant dealings with foreign powers would invite conflict and hurt American trade. Tit-for-tat tariffs after collapsed talks already strain that peace; renaming a lake both nations have called Ontario for centuries adds insult where calm heads and clear laws are needed.

No founding statute, treaty power, or early practice supports a president rewriting maps by executive order. The First Congress passed the Tariff Act of 1789 and organized the militia by statute; they did not let the president redraw boundaries or names on his own. This move treats Article II like a general grant of power instead of the limited executive the framers designed. It sets a bad example that invites other countries to treat American names and borders the same casual way.

The right way is for Congress to debate any change that touches foreign territory or shared waters, then ratify a treaty if Canada agrees.
