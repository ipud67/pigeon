Federal judge says DHS attempted to silence NY man’s speech following critical email

A man in New York sent an email to the former director of Immigration and Customs Enforcement in which he compared the director to a Nazi leader and wrote that the director would not know peace until death. The email came after the man grew angry over federal immigration operations and the killing of Alex Pretti in Minneapolis. Federal agents from the Department of Homeland Security visited the man's home on June 23 while he was on vacation and handed his wife a warning notice that said his actions could lead to federal or state .

On September 16 U.S. District Court Judge Rudolph Contreras ruled that the notice threatened the man with prosecution if he did not stop criticizing ICE and therefore violated his First right to free speech. The judge granted part of the man's motion for a preliminary and barred the department from relying on the notice, issuing similar threats or carrying out those threats. He rejected the government's request that the man first spell out exactly when and what he planned to say next.

The ruling now blocks the department from using that notice against the man. The Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression represents him in the suit. The same two agents delivered a similar order to another person in the Syracuse area during the state's primary election.

Constitutional analysis
A federal judge ruled that Department of Homeland Security agents violated a New York man’s First Amendment rights by delivering a warning notice after he sent a critical email to a former ICE director. The notice told David Streever to stop his criticism or face prosecution. The court granted part of his request for a preliminary injunction against the agency relying on that notice, issuing similar threats, or acting on them. The First Amendment protects the right of the people to speak against government officials and their actions. The founders wrote it to guard exactly this kind of sharp dissent. They had lived under British officials who used threats of prosecution to quiet opposition before the Revolution. Government exists first to secure the unalienable rights listed in the Declaration of Independence, beginning with the right to life. The killing of Alex Pretti in Minneapolis that angered Streever is the kind of event that makes plain why the people must be free to criticize how officials handle law, order, and public safety. The Preamble names domestic tranquility as a core purpose of the new Constitution so citizens can live in peace under equal laws. The founding generation knew that vague warnings from federal agents could chill speech as surely as direct censorship. James Madison in Federalist 43 warned against any construction of federal power that would let the government stretch its reach into areas reserved to the people. The First Amendment stands as one of those clear limits. The judge’s order blocks further use of the notice but does not reach the separate question of whether the agents had authority under any statute to investigate the email as a threat. That inquiry turns on the exact words used and the line between protected criticism and unprotected true threats, a line the founding generation drew in the Sedition Act debates of 1798 even while they punished false and malicious writings.
Thinking ahead
A federal judge in New York ruled on September 16 that a warning notice delivered by DHS agents to David Streever after his critical email to a former ICE director violated the First Amendment. The notice told Streever to stop his criticism or face possible prosecution. The court issued a preliminary injunction that blocks DHS from using that notice, sending similar warnings, or acting on its threats. This case sits on a ladder of prior incidents where federal agents delivered comparable notices to individuals voicing criticism of immigration enforcement, including one to poll worker Paigelynne Gonyea in Syracuse earlier this year. The injunction lasts until the full lawsuit reaches final judgment. Streever’s legal team from the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression will push for permanent relief and fees. DHS can appeal the ruling to the D.C. Circuit or adjust its internal procedures for handling complaints against employees. The situation changes only if an appeals court reverses the injunction or if DHS drops further enforcement attempts tied to the original notice. Next step likely stays inside the district court for discovery on the agents’ conduct and any pattern of similar notices. Likely to run another 12 to 18 months in litigation before settlement or final order.
