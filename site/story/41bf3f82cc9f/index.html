Supreme Court dismisses immigration case that was scheduled for argument in October

The Supreme Court has heard immigration detention cases for decades under the Immigration and Nationality Act. Section 1226(c) requires the government to hold certain noncitizens who committed listed crimes while their removal cases move forward. The law allows release only on narrow witness-protection grounds. In 2018 the justices ruled in Jennings v. Rodriguez that the itself does not require periodic bond hearings. The court left open whether the Fifth due process clause ever requires one after detention grows long.

On June 15 the court agreed to hear Genalo v. Black, which asked exactly when such a hearing must occur and what the government must show to keep holding the person. The case centered on Keisy G.M., detained 21 months after an assault conviction. While the petition was pending, one original petitioner left the country. G.M. himself was released from custody in 2022. In late August New York Governor Kathy Hochul pardoned his assault conviction.

The pardon removed the conviction that had made G.M. deportable and subject to mandatory detention under Section 1226(c). On Friday the Solicitor General notified the court that the case no longer presented a live controversy. The challenger agreed. The justices dismissed the case from the October argument calendar. The government said it will return with the same question in a new petition.

Constitutional analysis
The Supreme Court exists to secure the rights named in the Declaration, beginning with the right to life, and to maintain the domestic Tranquility the Preamble lists as the first purpose of government. That requires clear laws enforced at the border and inside the country so citizens can live in safety. The case Genalo v. Black asked whether certain criminal noncitizens detained under Section 1226(c) of the Immigration and Nationality Act gain a constitutional right to a bond hearing after months or years in custody. The justices had granted review to settle the due-process question left open in Jennings v. Rodriguez in 2018. On September 12 the Court dismissed the case as moot after one petitioner left the country and the other received a state pardon that ended his deportability. The Solicitor General told the justices the government will return with a new case to resolve the same statutory and constitutional limits on mandatory detention of criminal aliens. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to establish a uniform rule of naturalization and to make all laws necessary and proper for carrying into execution that power. The founding generation treated control of who enters and remains in the country as a core sovereign duty, not a judicially managed service with endless hearings. The dismissal leaves the Second Circuit’s extension of Fifth Amendment protections in place for now, but the government’s stated plan to relitigate the issue keeps the core question alive under the same constitutional framework the founders used when they entrusted immigration policy to the political branches.
