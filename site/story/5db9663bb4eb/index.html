A California man was arrested on a three-count federal indictment for smuggling more than 300 million dollars in export-controlled computer servers with US GPUs to China

The United States has barred the export of certain high-performance computer servers containing advanced graphics processing units to China without a license from the Department of since 2022. Those rules list the specific models that can contribute to military applications and require exporters to know the true end user and final destination before any sale. Earthmade Computer Inc., a company based in the City of Industry east of Los Angeles, bought and shipped such servers during that period.

On September 29 a federal grand jury in Los Angeles returned a three-count against Greg Lui, the 38-year-old owner of Earthmade. The charges are to violate the Export Control Reform Act and its regulations, outbound , and conspiracy to commit money laundering. Prosecutors say Lui and others bought more than $300 million worth of the restricted servers from U.S. makers between 2023 and 2024, sent false end-user statements claiming the items would stay in countries that need no license, routed the shipments through Malaysia and Singapore, and then moved them on to buyers in China. From January to October 2024 the company received more than $176 million from two Malaysia-based freight firms as payment for the diverted goods.

Lui was arrested in California on October 1 and appeared in federal court the same day for arraignment. The case now turns on whether prosecutors can prove he knew the ultimate destination was China and that the paperwork he supplied to manufacturers was false.

Greg Lui, 38, owner of Earthmade Computer Inc. in City of Industry, California, got arrested on a three-count federal indictment for smuggling over $300 million in high-end servers packed with U.S.-made GPUs used for super intelligence computing. The scheme ran from 2023 into 2024: his company bought the restricted gear from American makers, shipped it through Malaysia and Singapore where licenses were not needed, then rerouted it to end users in China while filing false paperwork that hid the real destination. This marks the latest step in U.S. enforcement of export rules on advanced chips that took effect after 2022 restrictions tightened controls on items that could boost other nations' military computing power. The case now sits with federal prosecutors in the Central District of California. Lui faces an initial court appearance and arraignment this afternoon. From here the process runs on standard federal timelines: discovery of evidence, possible plea negotiations, and trial if no deal is reached. How long this runs depends on whether Lui pleads or fights the charges, and whether co-conspirators or overseas partners get named in follow-on actions. The situation changes only if the Department of Commerce updates the export control list or if courts rule on what counts as sufficient proof of knowledge and false statements. No indicators yet point to either shift. Next likely step is pretrial motions and document unsealing that lay out the full chain of payments and emails already referenced in the indictment. Horizon: 12 to 24 months to resolution.
