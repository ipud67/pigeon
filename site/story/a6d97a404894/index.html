Gulf crude exports returned to pre-war levels in September as Iranian exports stayed low

Gulf crude oil exports averaged roughly 18 million barrels per day before the US and Israel opened strikes on Iran in late February 2026. The fight narrowed to control of the Strait of Hormuz, the 21-mile passage that carries much of the world's seaborne oil. Iran closed the strait after the Islamabad collapsed on 8 July, and the IRGC moved to shut it on 12 July.

In September Gulf producers sent non-Iranian crude back to and above the pre-war average on four days in the final week, with daily flows reaching between 19.5 million and 22.5 million barrels. They routed about 40 percent of the crude around Hormuz through alternative pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman, up from 17 percent before the war. Saudi Arabia stepped up Red Sea shipments while the UAE increased use of its pipeline to Fujairah. More than 70 percent of the crude changed tankers offshore in the Gulf of Oman in August, compared with almost none before the war.

Iranian exports stayed well below the pre-war average. At least seven attacks on vessels have been reported around Hormuz in recent weeks, including a projectile strike on the tanker Kazimah III on 1 October that forced the crew to evacuate. The volume of Gulf crude leaving the region now turns on continued use of the bypass routes.

Thinking ahead
Gulf crude exports hit pre-war levels again in September after a 72% drop in the seven-day average when Hormuz first closed in the early weeks of the US-Iran fight. Shippers now route 40% of the crude around the strait, up from 17% before the war, using Red Sea pipelines, the UAE Fujairah line, and ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman where over 70% of cargoes changed tankers in August. Iran’s own exports stay below the pre-war average because of US policy, while seven more vessel attacks have hit near Hormuz including the Kazimah III tanker struck on October 1. This pattern has run since the July 12 IRGC push to shut the strait and the collapse of the June Islamabad Memorandum. The shift to bypass routes has held steady for three months now. It will likely continue as long as tanker operators keep the new paths open and Gulf producers maintain the extra pipeline and transfer volumes. Next moves depend on whether the volume of bypass traffic stays above 40% or if renewed attacks force more ships back through Hormuz. No market figures from the supplied Treasury yields and spreads bear directly on the export recovery. Three to six months of current bypass levels.
