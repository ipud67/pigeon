TATARSTAN
September 17, 2026 · 08:40 UTC

Up to 25,000 North Koreans work in Russian drone production

Russia has brought in foreign labor to staff factories inside its military supply chain since it began the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. That move created labor shortages in industry as Russian men were called up or left the country. North Korea has sent workers abroad for decades under state-controlled programs that funnel most of their wages back to the government in Pyongyang. The two countries expanded cooperation on evasion and labor transfers after the invasion began, with Russia ranking second only to China in the number of North Korean workers it hosts.

A new report released on September 17 by the Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team states that Russia now uses as many as 25,000 North Korean workers at the Alabuga Special Economic Zone in Tatarstan. The workers entered the country on student visas instead of work permits and were assigned to produce Geran-2 drones for the Russian military. Construction of a large residential complex at Alabuga to house the foreign laborers finished in 2025. The report places the overall number of North Koreans in Russia between 15,000 and 30,000, most in construction and agriculture, with up to 1,000 in weapons-related plants.

The deployment has given the Alabuga site a steady supply of low-cost labor inside a heavily sanctioned drone factory while routing roughly $7,500 per worker each year back to the North Korean state. The arrangement now operates through a network of 70 to 80 DPRK companies and third-country recruiters, with the scale of the program tied directly to the demands of sustained wartime production.

Thinking ahead
Russia has brought North Korean workers into the Alabuga SEZ on student visas since 2025 to staff drone lines that supply forces in Ukraine. The latest MSMT sanctions report puts the headcount at up to 25,000 across 70-80 DPRK-linked firms, with roughly 1,000 of them inside weapons plants. The arrangement runs on visa loopholes, third-country recruiters, and a completed 2025 housing block built for the influx. It will keep running as long as the labor pipeline from Pyongyang stays open and Russian factories need bodies more than they fear added sanctions listings. Next visible step is either fresh MSMT updates that track visa numbers or new designations that hit the specific Alabuga subcontractors. The program shrinks only if visa rules tighten enough to cut the student flow or if direct North Korean troop commitments pull workers into combat roles instead. Horizon: at least another 12-18 months at current scale.
