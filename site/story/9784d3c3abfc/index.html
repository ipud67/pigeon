Judge: Illinois prescription drug ‘affordability’ law contradicts federal rules

A 2024 Illinois law required pharmacy benefit managers and other entities that manage prescription drug benefits for insurers to report detailed cost and pricing data to a new state affordability board. The law gave the board power to set upper payment limits on certain drugs. On September 2 2026 a federal judge blocked the law after finding it almost certainly conflicts with the federal Employee Retirement Income Security Act which governs employer-sponsored health plans and limits state rules on those plans. The ruling puts the Illinois mandates on hold.

Constitutional analysis
Illinois' Prescription Drug Affordability Act runs against the founding principle of limited government and the consent of the governed. The federal judge correctly blocked the state law because it piles new reporting mandates on private organizations that manage drug benefits for insurers. That overreach clashes with federal rules already on the books. The founders set up a system of enumerated powers so government would stay small and leave people free to handle their own affairs. James Madison warned in Federalist 45 that the powers of the federal government are “few and defined,” and the states were meant to handle the rest without inventing new burdens on commerce and contract. The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states and the people all powers not given to Washington, but it does not give states license to ignore federal law or harass private groups trying to keep costs down. The right to life comes first in the founding order. Government exists to secure life, liberty, and property so families can live in peace. When lawmakers force extra red tape on drug-benefit managers, they raise the cost of medicine that keeps people alive. The Illinois scheme treats private enterprise as an arm of the state instead of respecting the liberty the Declaration says belongs to every person. The Tariff Act of 1789 and the early commerce laws show the founding generation wanted trade and contracts to flow freely, not strangled by endless paperwork. States have real authority under federalism, but they cannot use it to contradict federal statutes or trample the settled expectations that let markets deliver life-saving drugs. This ruling reminds us why the founders divided power: to stop any one level of government from growing too big and burdensome. The people did not consent to a regulatory thicket that drives up the price of staying alive. Illinois lawmakers should scrap the law and let families, doctors, and insurers manage their own health decisions without extra mandates from politicians.
Thinking ahead
A federal judge blocked Illinois’ Prescription Drug Affordability Act because it piles extra reporting rules on pharmacy benefit managers that clash with federal ERISA standards. The ruling treats the law as likely preempted and puts it on hold while the case moves forward. Illinois can appeal to the 7th Circuit or rewrite the statute to drop the conflicting mandates. Without one of those two moves the law stays shelved. Next rung is either an appeal filing or legislative tweaks in Springfield. Horizon: 12–18 months until appellate ruling or revised bill.
