19:T7e9,Illinois' Prescription Drug Affordability Act runs against the founding principle of limited government and the consent of the governed.

The federal judge correctly blocked the state law because it piles new reporting mandates on private organizations that manage drug benefits for insurers. That overreach clashes with federal rules already on the books. The founders set up a system of enumerated powers so government would stay small and leave people free to handle their own affairs. James Madison warned in Federalist 45 that the powers of the federal government are “few and defined,” and the states were meant to handle the rest without inventing new burdens on commerce and contract. The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states and the people all powers not given to Washington, but it does not give states license to ignore federal law or harass private groups trying to keep costs down.

The right to life comes first in the founding order. Government exists to secure life, liberty, and property so families can live in peace. When lawmakers force extra red tape on drug-benefit managers, they raise the cost of medicine that keeps people alive. The Illinois scheme treats private enterprise as an arm of the state instead of respecting the liberty the Declaration says belongs to every person. The Tariff Act of 1789 and the early commerce laws show the founding generation wanted trade and contracts to flow freely, not strangled by endless paperwork. States have real authority under federalism, but they cannot use it to contradict federal statutes or trample the settled expectations that let markets deliver life-saving drugs.

This ruling reminds us why the founders divided power: to stop any one level of government from growing too big and burdensome. The people did not consent to a regulatory thicket that drives up the price of staying alive.
