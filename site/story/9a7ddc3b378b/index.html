Citing election-fraud concerns, Trump administration brings dispute over voter database to the Supreme Court

The Department of Homeland Security has run a database called SAVE since the 1980s to let federal and state agencies check whether applicants for public benefits are non-citizens. On March 25 2026 President Trump ordered DHS and the Social Security to expand that database so state election officials could search it in bulk to confirm voters’ and immigration status. The agencies added records on U.S.-born citizens, linked Social Security numbers, and changed the system to handle group queries instead of one name at a time.

On June 22 U.S. District Judge Sparkle Sooknanan ruled that the expanded SAVE violated the Privacy Act, the Social Security Act, and federal law. She blocked the government from letting states use the new version. The D.C. Circuit refused to stay that order while the appeal continued, though it sped up the case. On September 8 the Trump administration asked the Supreme Court to pause the district judge’s so the database could be used for voter checks during the current election cycle.

The case now sits before the justices along with a separate emergency request filed three days earlier over a Boston judge’s order on mail-in ballot rules. The D.C. appeal on the SAVE changes remains on a fast track in the lower court.

Trump administration went back to the Supreme Court Tuesday for the second time in three days. This time they want the justices to freeze a June 22 order from a D.C. federal judge that blocks the updated SAVE database from checking voter citizenship. The D.C. Circuit already turned down a stay but sped up the appeal. The ladder runs from the March 25 executive order that told DHS and SSA to build bulk-check tools, through the database overhaul that added U.S.-born records and SSN access, to the League of Women Voters lawsuit, the judge’s injunction, and the appeals-court denial. Next move sits with the justices. They can grant the stay, which would let states use the current SAVE version right away, or deny it and keep the block in place while the D.C. Circuit hears the full appeal on a fast track. A grant would shift the practical fight to state election offices; a denial would keep the database locked until the appeals court rules or the Supreme Court takes the case on the merits.
