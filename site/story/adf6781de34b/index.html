Two lawsuits in California seek to recover Nazi-looted art from museums

California museums have held certain artworks for decades after their ownership passed through sales and government decisions in the years right after World War II. In the Netherlands the government took possession of two Lucas Cranach the Elder oil paintings that had belonged to Dutch Jewish collector Jacques Goudstikker before his family fled the country in 1940. The Dutch transferred those paintings in the 1960s to George Stroganoff-Scherbatoff, who sold them to the Norton Simon Museum in Pasadena in 1971. A separate set of watercolor portraits painted by Dina Gottliebova Babbitt while imprisoned at Auschwitz in 1944 has remained at the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum in Poland since the war.

On September 17, 2026 the Jewish Federation Los Angeles filed suit in California state court against the Norton Simon Museum to recover the two Cranach paintings. The Federation received the claim from the sole surviving Goudstikker heir, Marei von Saher, who had lost a 2007 federal case against the same museum in 2018 when the Ninth Circuit ruled that U.S. courts could not undo the Dutch government’s postwar transfer. The new suit relies on a 2024 California law that lets state residents pursue property taken during political persecution and on the federal Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery Act, which bars foreign government actions from blocking recovery of Nazi-looted art. A second suit filed the same week in federal court in Los Angeles seeks return of the Babbitt watercolors from the Polish state museum.

The lawsuits place the paintings’ legal title back before California courts and the Norton Simon Museum. The Federation has said it will donate most of any money received from the Cranachs to support Holocaust survivors living in poverty in the Los Angeles area.

Thinking ahead
Two lawsuits filed this week in California federal and state courts target paintings held by the Norton Simon Museum in Pasadena and the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum in Poland. The first suit, brought by the Jewish Federation Los Angeles after receiving assignment of claims from heir Marei von Saher, relies on a 2024 state law that lets California residents pursue items taken during political persecution and on a federal statute that blocks foreign government acts as a defense in Nazi-looted art cases. That claim follows a 2007 federal suit by von Saher that ended in 2018 when the Ninth Circuit ruled courts could not override the Dutch government's postwar transfer of the Lucas Cranach the Elder works. The second suit seeks watercolors made by Auschwitz prisoner Dina Gottliebova Babbitt under orders from Josef Mengele. Both cases now sit at the motion-to-dismiss stage, where museums will argue statutes of limitations, prior rulings, and foreign sovereign immunity. The Federation has said it will donate most proceeds from any recovery to aid Holocaust survivors. Progress turns on whether the new statutes clear the procedural bars that stopped the 2007 case and on whether Polish museum officials respond in U.S. court. The suits will likely take 18 to 36 months to reach any trial or settlement, driven by discovery fights over provenance documents and foreign-government defenses. Next step is the museums' answers or dismissal motions, due in coming weeks. Horizon: rulings on motions by late 2027.
