CANADA
September 10, 2026 · 17:35 UTC

Canada and Ukraine to scale up drone production, build up Canada’s defence industries, and achieve a just and lasting peace in Ukraine

Canada has trained and equipped Ukrainian forces since Russia seized Crimea in 2014. Operation UNIFIER began that year at Ukraine’s request and expanded after the full-scale invasion in February 2022. Canada has since committed more than 26 billion dollars in military, economic, and reconstruction aid, including air-defence systems, energy repairs and on more than 3,500 Russian-linked individuals and entities. The training mission was extended in February 2026 for three more years, to 2029.

On 10 September 2026 Prime Minister Mark Carney hosted President Volodymyr Zelenskyy for his fifth visit to Canada. The two leaders signed a 100-year partnership declaration and a joint defence-cooperation declaration that set up government-to-government work on uncrewed systems, counter-drone tools and munitions. They launched the Defence Drone Initiative Marketplace, a digital platform modelled on Ukraine’s Brave1 that links the Canadian Armed Forces and Coast Guard to nearly 400 domestic suppliers. Initial contracts worth up to 50 million dollars will increase the number of drones in Canadian service tenfold and require all training, maintenance and sustainment work to occur inside Canada. A new production tie-up between General Dynamics Mission Systems-Canada and Ukraine’s Green Tech Harvest will cover everything from critical minerals to final assembly.

The announcements also added roughly 350 million dollars for air-defence interceptors through the U.S.-led JUMPSTART program, 435 million dollars in loan guarantees for energy security and winter gas purchases, 200 million dollars in concessional loans for reconstruction through Export Development Canada, and up to 40.1 million dollars for Ukrainian veterans plus 6 million dollars to trace and reintegrate children taken by Russia. Total Canadian support for Ukraine now stands above 26 billion dollars since 2022. The new agreements turn on scaling Canadian factories, supply chains and joint production lines while Ukraine supplies battlefield data on drone employment.

Constitutional analysis
Canada’s announcement of new drone production partnerships with Ukraine and $26 billion in total aid since 2022 raises the first question the founders asked of any foreign engagement: does it serve the security of the American republic and the unalienable right to life of its own people. The Constitution grants Congress the power to provide for the common defence and to raise and support armies under Article I, Section 8, but it limits that power to enumerated ends and reserves to the states and the people all authority not expressly delegated. Washington himself put down the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 with militia called under the Militia Acts of 1792, showing that the federal government exists to secure domestic tranquility and protect American lives and property first. Foreign alliances and expenditures must never invert that order or create permanent entanglements that drain treasure and attention from the nation’s own borders and armed forces. The Defence Drone Initiative Marketplace and contracts with Canadian firms such as Beonyx, AVSS, Volatus, Draganfly, and Objexis aim to build sovereign Canadian capacity for uncrewed systems. This follows the pattern of the Tariff Act of 1789, which funded the new government by protecting domestic industry so it could sustain independent defence. Co-production deals with Ukrainian firms like Green Tech Harvest and joint declarations for a 100-year partnership extend that model across borders, committing Canada to long-term defence industrial cooperation, critical-minerals research, and munitions development. Additional spending includes $350 million for air-defence interceptors through the U.S.-led JUMPSTART program, $435 million in loan guarantees for Ukrainian energy infrastructure, $200 million in concessional loans for reconstruction, and $40.1 million for veterans and deported children. These measures rest on the claim that bolstering Ukraine deters future threats to Canada and Europe. The founders warned in The Federalist No. 4 and No. 8 that standing alliances and distant quarrels breed standing armies, consolidated power, and taxes that erode the consent of the governed at home. Canada’s choice to model its drone marketplace on Ukraine’s Brave1 and to scale output to millions of units over two years accelerates exactly the permanent defence bureaucracy the Constitution was written to constrain. The joint statement and two declarations signed this week formalize the roadmap. Whether these steps ultimately strengthen or weaken the limited federal republic the Americans built in 1787 will be measured by one test the founders left us: does the policy secure the lives, liberties, and property of our own citizens first, or does it treat that duty as secondary to foreign causes.
