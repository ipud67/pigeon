The Justice Department sued two lab executives in Atlanta for schemes that allegedly funneled millions in Medicare payments for unnecessary genetic and respiratory tests ordered through kickbacks at church events and senior living facilities. The complaint claims they used copied physician signatures, standing orders, and sales staff instead of real doctor judgment to trigger the claims.

Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to lay and collect taxes and to provide for the general welfare, but the founders understood this as limited spending on objects truly common to all, not open-ended programs ripe for fraud. The Federalist No. 41 warned that loose construction of the spending power would swallow every other limit and turn the government into an unlimited one. Medicare, created by statute in 1965, operates under that power, yet the alleged fraud shows how easily such programs invite abuse when kickbacks replace individualized medical decisions.

The founders knew fraud against the public purse struck at the consent of the governed. Washington led troops to put down the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 when citizens refused a tax they saw as unjustly collected and spent. The First Congress passed the Tariff Act of 1789 to raise revenue honestly and the Militia Acts to secure domestic tranquility. Suing to recover funds taken through false claims follows that tradition of protecting the treasury that funds the government's core duties, starting with the protection of life and property.

The case rests on the False Claims Act, rooted in Civil War-era efforts to stop contractors from cheating the government. It seeks recovery from the executives, the ex-wife who allegedly received transferred funds, and related entities. Earlier settlements with the lab and its billing company totaled over $14 million. 1b:T596,DOJ filed a civil complaint in federal court against Jay Johnson and Austin Whiles, the two executives who ran Capstone Diagnostics in Atlanta. The suit claims they ran two kickback schemes that sent millions in Medicare claims for genetic tests at church events and respiratory panels in senior living facilities, with paperwork signed by doctors who never saw the patients. The government already settled with the lab itself and its owner for $14.3 million and with the billing company for $300,000. Johnson faces a separate criminal indictment from December 2025 on conspiracy to commit health care fraud tied to the church side; that case is still pending. The civil complaint also names his ex-wife Sarah Haslock and several companies as targets for money-recovery claims.

The case sits at the complaint stage, so the next visible steps are service on the defendants, their answer or motion to dismiss, and whatever discovery the government turns over from the earlier qui tam filing. How long it runs depends on whether the parties settle fast like the lab did or fight every document request and expert report. The criminal track against Johnson moves on its own schedule in the Northern District of Georgia; a conviction there would hand the civil side a strong collateral-estoppel card.

Without more filings or a trial date on the record, the indicators stay thin.
