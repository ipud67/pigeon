NEW YORK
September 25, 2026 · 13:00 UTC

United States prosecutors charged Uzbek national Davud Ansariy in New York with leading a Brooklyn fundraising network that sent at least three fighters to Syria

Uzbek nationals who became naturalized U.S. citizens lived in Brooklyn and formed groups that collected money from 2013 onward to pay for plane tickets and family support for men who wanted to reach Syria. The collections operated under the name chayxona, the Uzbek term for tea house, and the participants sent at least three travelers that year. All three later died in Syria. One participant left the United States for Turkey on a one-way ticket in May 2016, five days after federal agents arrested a co-conspirator in the same network. A federal grand jury in Brooklyn returned an against that man in November 2021 on charges of conspiracy and provision of material support to the Islamic State of Iraq and al-Sham and to the Al-Nusrah Front, both designated foreign terrorist organizations during the period covered by the charges.

Davud Ansariy, also known as Kozimjon Gafurov and Kozim, was arrested in Tbilisi, Georgia, in August 2026 and extradited from there to the Eastern District of New York on September 23.

He appeared this afternoon in federal court in Brooklyn before Judge Lara K. Eshkenazi for arraignment on the 2021 indictment. The case was investigated by the FBI with assistance from the New York Police Department, New York State Police, U.S. Customs and Border Protection, Port Authority Police Department and U.S. Marshals Service. The Department of Justice Office of International Affairs, the FBI legal attache in Tbilisi and Georgian prosecutors handled the arrest and transfer.

Constitutional analysis
A Uzbek national and naturalized U.S. citizen named Davud Ansariy, also known as Kozimjon Gafurov or Kozim, was extradited from Georgia and arrived in the United States to face federal charges. He will be arraigned in the Eastern District of New York in Brooklyn on one count of conspiracy and one count of providing material support to ISIS and the Al-Nusrah Front, both designated foreign terrorist organizations when the conduct occurred. The indictment returned by a federal grand jury in November 2021 alleges that Ansariy led a Brooklyn-based network called “chayxona,” or tea house, that raised money to send at least three fighters to Syria in 2013, funded travel and family support for those who joined, and continued operations after Ansariy left the country for Turkey in May 2016. The charges carry a maximum penalty of 35 years in prison if he is convicted. The case is prosecuted under the authority of Article III, Section 1 of the Constitution, which vests the judicial power of the United States in federal courts to try offenses against the laws of the United States. Congress defined material support to foreign terrorist organizations in statutes enacted pursuant to its enumerated powers in Article I, Section 8 to punish offenses against the law of nations, define and punish piracies and felonies on the high seas, and provide for the common defense. The founders viewed protection against foreign threats as a core purpose of government, stated in the Preamble as providing for the common defence and securing the Blessings of Liberty, and in the Declaration as the first object of instituting government to secure unalienable rights including life. George Washington invoked the 1792 Militia Acts and personally led militia in 1794 to suppress the Whiskey Rebellion and restore domestic tranquility when domestic groups defied federal law. The extradition from Georgia rests on treaties made under the president’s Article II treaty power with the advice and consent of the Senate. The specific clause or pending ruling that would settle any dispute over the reach of the material-support statute is the text of 18 U.S.C. § 2339B itself together with the jury’s verdict on the facts alleged in the indictment.
