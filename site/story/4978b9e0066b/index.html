US court dismisses 4chan's case against Britain's media regulator

Britain’s Online Safety Act gave its media regulator Ofcom power to investigate and fine internet platforms that failed to prevent certain illegal content from reaching UK users. The law took effect in stages after Parliament passed it in 2023. Ofcom began sending notices to operators abroad when it found apparent breaches.

On August 12 2025 Ofcom issued 4chan a provisional notice of contravention. The notice warned of an initial £20 000 fine plus daily penalties of £100 for up to 60 days. 4chan and the forum Kiwi Farms filed suit in Washington federal court later that month. They argued the regulator’s letters interfered with their operations and US rights.

A US district judge dismissed the case on September 16 2026. The ruling said the court lacked because the companies could defend themselves in British courts. Any judgment Ofcom obtained would still have to be enforced in the United States where the platforms have no physical presence. The judge wrote that the suit tried to use American courts as a sword rather than a shield.

Constitutional analysis
A U.S. district court in Washington dismissed a lawsuit filed by 4chan and Kiwi Farms against Britain’s media regulator Ofcom. The sites had argued that Ofcom’s enforcement of the UK Online Safety Act, including a provisional notice of contravention and fines, violated their rights and operations. Judge Rudolph Contreras ruled the court lacked jurisdiction because the dispute centers on British law applied to British users. He noted the platforms could defend themselves in British courts and raise any enforcement issues if Ofcom later sought to collect a judgment in the United States. The First Amendment protects speech from federal laws made by Congress, as the founders intended when they barred prior restraints and allowed robust debate even on unpopular or offensive topics. The Federalist No. 84 explains that the Constitution grants the national government only specific powers, leaving the rest to the states or the people. Article I, Section 8 lists those powers and contains no authority for Congress to regulate foreign speech rules or police content on foreign soil. The founders rejected standing armies and entangling alliances in part to avoid exactly this kind of overseas regulatory reach that could drag Americans into foreign legal fights. The right to life comes first in the founding order, but here the case turns on enumerated powers and the limits of American courts acting as a sword rather than a shield against foreign regulators. The decision leaves the British regulator free to proceed under its own statute while requiring any future collection effort to clear U.S. hurdles.
