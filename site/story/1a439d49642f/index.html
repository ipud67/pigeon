October 1, 2026 · 06:56 UTC

Tennessee tried twice to execute death row inmate Christa Pike with lethal injection but she lived

Tennessee has carried out executions by lethal injection since it reinstated the death penalty in 1976. The state sets the three-drug protocol that begins with a sedative followed by a paralytic and a heart-stopping agent, and it schedules the date, place, and witnesses once all appeals and clemency requests are exhausted.

On October 1 the execution team at Riverbend Maximum Security Institution in Nashville began the process on Christa Pike, who has been on death row since 1996 for the murder of a fellow student. After the first drug was administered the medical staff could not locate a usable vein for the remaining injections, and the warden halted the procedure before the full sequence could be completed.

The attempt ended with Pike returned to her cell and the state required to decide whether to schedule a new date, pursue different execution methods now allowed under Tennessee , or face further court review of the failed procedure.

Constitutional analysis
Tennessee carried out the first step of a death sentence against Christa Pike on October 1 2026. The state acted under its authority to punish murder after a jury convicted her in 1995 of killing a fellow inmate at the Tennessee Prison for Women. The execution attempt failed when the lethal injection process could not proceed. The right to life named first in the Declaration of Independence as the reason governments are instituted among men stands at the center of capital punishment. The Fifth Amendment to the Constitution permits the government to deprive a person of life provided it follows due process of law. The First Congress in 1790 made murder a capital offense punishable by death after conviction in federal court. Tennessee followed that pattern when it set the punishment for first-degree murder. The courts will now decide the next step under the statutes that govern sentences of death.
