KAMCHATKA PENINSULA
September 24, 2026 · 15:47 UTC

US-India Satellite Captures Time-lapse Video of Volcanic Eruption

The Kamchatka Peninsula in far eastern Russia holds more than 150 volcanoes along the Pacific Ring of Fire. One pair called Krasheninnikov last produced a recorded eruption around the year 1550. Ground instruments track many of the peninsula’s active cones because they erupt often, yet Krasheninnikov stayed quiet for nearly five centuries until an 8.8-magnitude offshore earthquake on July 30 2025 shook the seabed nearby.

The northern crater of the pair began to erupt a few days after that quake. Lava has flowed eastward in a steady field since then. The joint NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar satellite, operating at 747 kilometers altitude, passed over the site on December 25 2025 while completing its post-launch checks. It has since returned twice every 12 days, once south-to-north and once north-to-south, recording radar images at roughly 10-meter resolution.

Researchers assembled 17 of those frames through mid-August 2026 into a time-lapse sequence released on September 24 2026. The sequence shows lava first filling an inner caldera, then spilling into the wider outer crater and spreading into a fan shape across the landscape. The satellite’s L-band radar has kept the same viewing geometry on every pass, allowing direct comparison of surface change at that fixed scale.

Thinking ahead
The US-India NISAR satellite has put together a time-lapse from 17 radar passes over Krasheninnikov volcano on Russia's Kamchatka Peninsula. The northern crater started spilling lava after an 8.8-magnitude offshore quake on July 30 2025, the first eruption there since around 1550. Each pass catches the flow filling the inner basin then pushing east into a widening fan at 30-foot resolution. The satellite hits the same spot twice every 12 days in high-resolution mode from both northbound and southbound tracks. That repeat coverage has held steady from late December 2025 through mid-August 2026 and shows no sign of dropping off. Ground sensors on other Kamchatka volcanoes stay active but Krasheninnikov has none, so the orbital record is the main steady feed. The flow rate and direction stay visible in each frame, which lets teams track how far the lava field grows before it cools or meets a barrier. Next images will keep stacking on the same schedule unless the satellite drops a pass or the eruption stops sending fresh material. The record changes only if the vent shuts or the orbit shifts. This runs as long as the eruption supplies lava and the satellite keeps its 12-day cycle. Horizon: at least another 3-6 months of steady imaging.
