17:T4e8,The US-India NISAR satellite has put together a time-lapse from 17 radar passes over Krasheninnikov volcano on Russia's Kamchatka Peninsula. The northern crater started spilling lava after an 8.8-magnitude offshore quake on July 30 2025, the first eruption there since around 1550. Each pass catches the flow filling the inner basin then pushing east into a widening fan at 30-foot resolution.

The satellite hits the same spot twice every 12 days in high-resolution mode from both northbound and southbound tracks. That repeat coverage has held steady from late December 2025 through mid-August 2026 and shows no sign of dropping off. Ground sensors on other Kamchatka volcanoes stay active but Krasheninnikov has none, so the orbital record is the main steady feed.

The flow rate and direction stay visible in each frame, which lets teams track how far the lava field grows before it cools or meets a barrier. Next images will keep stacking on the same schedule unless the satellite drops a pass or the eruption stops sending fresh material. The record changes only if the vent shuts or the orbit shifts.

This runs as long as the eruption supplies lava and the satellite keeps its 12-day cycle.
