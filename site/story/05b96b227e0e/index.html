ABC News: Flydubai co-pilot previously suspended over extremist material

Translated from Dutch · read the original →
The original headlineABC News: copiloot Flydubai eerder geschorst vanwege extremistisch materiaal

The co-pilot of a Flydubai Boeing 737 held the rank of first officer and sat in the right-hand seat during commercial flights that carried passengers between the United Arab Emirates and other destinations. Airlines in the Gulf routinely screen crew for security and safety after a 2015 incident in which a Germanwings co-pilot locked his captain out of the cockpit and flew an Airbus A320 into a mountainside in the French Alps killing all 150 people on board. That event led carriers to tighten rules on crew background checks and access to the flight deck.

On Wednesday the same Flydubai co-pilot attacked the captain inside the cockpit of a flight that had just departed Dubai. The assault forced an emergency diversion and the aircraft landed safely. Investigators later established that the man had been suspended from a previous job at Oman Air after security personnel discovered extremist material in his possession. Sources who spoke to ABC News on condition of anonymity said the material prompted Oman Air to view him as a safety risk and bar him from flying.

The incident has prompted renewed checks across Gulf carriers on crew who transferred between airlines. Flydubai has not released the co-pilot’s name and the United Arab Emirates authorities have opened a criminal inquiry that now includes the earlier suspension at Oman Air. The case turns on how the man passed subsequent vetting and what access he retained to the cockpit.

Thinking ahead
The co-pilot on the Flydubai flight that saw an attack on the captain this week had been suspended earlier by Oman Air after extremist material turned up on him. Sources told ABC News the suspension flagged him as a safety risk at the time. Airlines and regulators now face checks on how that record carried over when he moved to Flydubai. This type of incident usually triggers an immediate probe by the airline, the civil aviation authority in the UAE, and the state where the pilot trained. Investigators pull logs, crew files, and any prior employer records. The case stays open until the final report lands, which often takes months. Next step is formal interviews with surviving crew and ground staff plus a review of the co-pilot’s devices and travel history. If the earlier suspension shows up in shared databases, watch for tightened rules on pilot background transfers between carriers. The situation changes only when the official investigation report names the exact trigger and any missed flags. Horizon: investigation runs at least six months.
