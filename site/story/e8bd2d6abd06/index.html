Israel welcomes sixth submarine as IDF chief warns Iran remains a threat

Israel and the United States began strikes on Iran in late February 2026. The fighting settled into weekly exchanges that month, spread to Lebanon, and produced naval clashes in the Strait of Hormuz. A June memorandum lifted the mutual blockades on shipping and opened a sixty-day negotiating window. It collapsed on 8 July, the United States resumed sustained nightly strikes, and the contest narrowed to control of the strait itself.

On 25 September the Israeli Navy took delivery of its sixth submarine at the Haifa Naval Base. The Dolphin II-class vessel, named INS Drakon, was built in Kiel by ThyssenKrupp Marine Systems and sailed from Germany. IDF Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir told the ceremony that Iran remains a threat even after the damage it has taken. He described the submarine’s arrival as one of the IDF’s central force-building steps and said it gives the navy a posture that is more covert, reaches farther, dives deeper, and carries greater striking power.

The induction expands the fleet’s operational range thousands of kilometers from Israel’s coast and adds to the assets already committed to the Hormuz theater. President Isaac Herzog, Defense Minister Israel Katz, and Zamir attended the handover. The navy now holds six German-built submarines, the largest produced in Germany since the Second World War.

Israel added its sixth Dolphin II-class diesel-electric attack submarine to the fleet at Haifa Naval Base on 25 September 2026. The boat, built by ThyssenKrupp in Kiel, Germany, carries air-independent propulsion that lets it stay submerged longer than earlier models and extends patrol range beyond the Mediterranean and Red Sea. IDF Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir spoke at the handover and listed Iran and its proxies as a standing threat that has taken hits but kept the same goals. The navy now holds six submarines, two more than it operated before the current round of fighting began in late February. This raises the number of hulls available for covert missions in the Strait of Hormuz or farther afield while the fight there stays locked on control of tanker routes. The induction marks one more step in a naval buildup that has run alongside the air and ground phases since the US and Israel opened strikes on 28 February. Weekly strike-and-counterstrike settled in by early March, the Islamabad Memorandum opened and then collapsed in July, and US nightly strikes resumed with focus on Iranian maritime sites and logistics. The new submarine does not change the immediate tactical picture in the strait but adds one more platform that can operate unseen for weeks. The situation will continue at current intensity until one side alters the naval posture in the strait or a new diplomatic opening appears. Next observable steps are either further Israeli or US strikes on Iranian naval infrastructure or an Iranian attempt to interdict more commercial traffic. Without a visible shift in mine-laying, patrol density, or tanker routing data, the narrow contest over Hormuz holds steady. Horizon: at least 30 days with no change in fleet posture or strait crossings.
