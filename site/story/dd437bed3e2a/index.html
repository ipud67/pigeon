WASHINGTON
September 28, 2026 · 15:16 UTC

Trump asked Chinese leader Xi Jinping if he would like to buy American weapons, US ambassador David Perdue said in the United States

The United States has maintained a legal ban on arms sales to China since Congress passed the relevant restrictions in the 1980s following the normalization of diplomatic relations with Beijing in 1979. That ban sits alongside a separate 1979 law that requires the US to provide Taiwan with weapons for its defense. The two have shaped every US administration's handling of weapons transfers across the Taiwan Strait for more than four decades.

On September 28 the State Department issued a statement to the New York Times declaring that US law prohibits arms sales to China and that no offer or plan to sell arms to China exists. The statement came one day after US Ambassador to China David Perdue told Fox News that President Trump had asked Chinese President Xi Jinping during Xi's state visit to the White House the previous week whether he would like to buy American weapons. Perdue said Trump made the remark in reply to Chinese complaints about US sales to Taiwan.

The White House and a US official separately confirmed to reporters that no sales to China are in the pipeline. The legal prohibition remains unchanged and continues to govern what weapons the executive branch may transfer.

Thinking ahead
The US State Department stated on September 28 that American law bars arms sales to China and that no such offer or plan exists. This followed remarks by Ambassador David Perdue on Fox News that President Trump had asked President Xi Jinping during last week's White House visit whether China wanted to buy US weapons, in reply to Beijing's complaints about sales to Taiwan. The White House and State Department both confirmed no sales pipeline to China. Perdue's account fits the pattern set by earlier Trump-Xi meetings this year, including Trump's May trip to Beijing and the December approval of an $11.1 billion arms package to Taiwan that Perdue called the largest since 1979. The May pause on a separate $14 billion Taiwan purchase, described by Trump as a negotiating chip, showed the same linkage between Taiwan arms and broader US-China talks. The situation stays frozen until Congress or the executive branch changes the law or policy that currently blocks sales to China. Next visible step would be any formal Chinese response beyond the foreign ministry's general statement on self-reliant defense, or a new US notification to Congress on Taiwan packages that Beijing then protests. This holds steady for the next several months of scheduled Trump-Xi meetings. Horizon: through APEC in Shenzhen.
