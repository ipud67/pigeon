America’s founders insisted that legitimate power comes only from the consent of the governed, expressed through regular, honest elections. The Federalist Papers, especially No. 49 and No. 78, treat peaceful transfer of power and the people’s verdict at the ballot box as the safeguard against tyranny. When elections turn tense, the real test is whether the people’s will is honored without violence or fraud.

Zambia’s story stands apart from American constitutional order. Our system limits federal power to enumerated objects, leaves most domestic matters to the states under the Tenth Amendment, and rejects standing armies in peacetime per the founders’ fear of concentrated force. Still, the core principle travels: government exists to protect life and property so free citizens can build their own prosperity, not to rule by decree or endless crisis.

Hichilema’s focus on economic turnaround echoes the early American emphasis on commerce and sound policy after the weak Articles of Confederation. The Tariff Act of 1789 and the First Bank debates showed the founders wanted government to clear the road for honest work, not to own the economy.
