18:T421,MONUSCO has backed the ceasefire monitoring setup in eastern DRC for years as part of its mandate to track armed group activity. On September 16 its head James Swan repeated that backing and called for more trucks, fuel, radios and trained staff so the teams can reach remote sites faster. The next procedural step is a joint meeting with the government and the other signatories to list exactly what gear and personnel gaps exist.  

Without visible delivery of those supplies the mechanism stays limited to main roads and known camps. If the extra logistical capacity shows up and the teams start filing regular verified reports from deeper field positions the ceasefire hold can be tested in more places. If the lists get written but the trucks and radios never arrive the whole setup stays on paper.  

The situation stays in place until one side visibly blocks the monitors or the monitors start producing public logs that one side rejects.  

Three-to-six months more of the same pattern unless the supply list turns into delivered gear on the
