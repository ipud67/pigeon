18:T5f5,The right to keep and bear arms stands as one of the core protections the founders wrote into the Bill of Rights so free citizens could defend their lives, their families, and the republic itself. The Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel has now concluded that 18 U.S.C. § 922(b)(1) and (c)(1), which bar licensed dealers from selling handguns to 18-to-20-year-olds, cannot be enforced against dealers selling to otherwise law-abiding adults in that age group. The opinion rests on the plain text of the Second Amendment, which protects “the people,” and on the historical record that 18-year-olds at the founding were treated as full members of the political community. They could vote in many states, sit on juries, face adult criminal penalties including death, and above all serve in the militia. The Militia Acts of 1792 required men to enroll at 18 and to furnish their own arms at their own expense. Federalist 29 and the debates around the Second Amendment itself tied the right to arms directly to that militia duty beginning at 18. No founding-era statute or practice barred legal adults from buying firearms for self-defense or militia service. The opinion therefore holds that these federal restrictions burden the core right of “the people” to acquire arms in common use and lack any supporting tradition from the founding period. 1b:T604,The Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel issued an opinion on September 18 2026 that the federal statutes blocking licensed dealers from selling handguns to 18-to-20-year-olds cannot be criminally enforced. The opinion rests on the Supreme Court’s rulings in Heller, Bruen, and Wolford, which set the test that 18-to-20-year-olds count as part of “the people” protected by the Second Amendment and that no founding-era laws barred legal adults from buying arms. It points to militia rules from the 1700s that started service at 18 and required each man to bring his own weapon.

This marks the latest step in a string of post-Bruen opinions that narrowed federal age restrictions on firearms. The opinion does not strike the statutes down; it directs prosecutors not to bring charges against dealers who sell to otherwise law-abiding buyers in that age group.

How long this holds depends on whether the next administration keeps the opinion in place or a court case forces the department to defend the ban. Without new legislation or a binding court order the other way, dealers can continue these sales and federal agents will treat the prohibition as unenforceable. The practical change is limited to licensed dealers; private sales and state laws stay untouched.

Next move is likely quiet adoption by field offices unless a lawsuit challenges the opinion directly or Congress passes a new statute that survives Second Amendment review.
