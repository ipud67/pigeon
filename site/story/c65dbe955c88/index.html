In final scheduled summer order list, Supreme Court again declines to weigh in on COVID-19 vaccine mandate case

“raises an important and recurring question of federal law that warrants this Court’s attention.”
Overview
The Supreme Court on Friday morning once again declined to weigh in on a case brought by New York healthcare workers who were denied a religious accommodation from the state’s COVID-19 vaccine mandate for healthcare workers and lost their jobs when they were not vaccinated. The denial of rehearing in Does 1-2 v. Hochul came as part of the third and final regularly scheduled list of summer orders. Summer order lists normally serve as a chance for the justices to clear out some of the pending requests for action – particularly petitions for rehearing – that build up between the end of June or early July, when they hold their final conference before their summer recess, and late September, when they return and hold the so-called “ long conference.” Although the court ordinarily does not add new cases to its merits docket on its summer order lists (as opposed to after the “long conference”), it sometimes opts to do so, as it did on July 20, when it granted review in Jouppi v. Alaska. In Does 1-2 v. Hochul, the court denied the healthcare workers’ original petition for review on June 29, over a dissent by Justice Neil Gorsuch that Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito joined. Gorsuch contended that the healthcare workers’ case “raises an important and recurring question of federal law that warrants this Court’s attention.” “I fail to see,” Gorsuch emphasized, “how a state law (especially an unconstitutional state law) prohibiting an accommodation can always and automatically supply an employer with a” defense that accommodating the employee’s religious observance or practice would place an “undue hardship” on the employer’s business. On July 23, the healthcare workers asked the Supreme Court to reconsider its denial of review. They argued that the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 2nd Circuit, whose decision the Supreme Court had left in place, has continued to adhere to its position that “federal antidiscrimination claims must yield to contrary state schemes.” Moreover, they added, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit has also “adopted the framework that religious accommodations must yield to contrary state laws,” which exacerbates the division among federal appeals courts on this question – an important factor that the Supreme Court considers in deciding whether to grant review. On Friday, the court turned down the healthcare workers’ petition for reconsideration without asking New York Governor Kathy Hochul to respond. The justices will meet on Sept. 28 for their “ long conference ”; they are likely to issue an initial list of orders from that conference later that week.
Short history
The Supreme Court first turned down review in Does 1-2 v. Hochul on June 29. Justices Gorsuch, Thomas, and Alito dissented, stating the case presented a recurring federal question on whether state vaccine rules can block religious accommodations under federal antidiscrimination law. The healthcare workers filed a petition for rehearing on July 23, citing ongoing splits between the 2nd and 9th Circuits on whether federal claims must yield to state statutes. On September 4 the court denied rehearing without requesting a response from New York. The denial came in the third and final scheduled summer order list, which clears pending rehearing requests before the justices return for their September 28 long conference.
Constitutional analysis
The Supreme Court on September 4 declined to rehear Does 1-2 v. Hochul, leaving in place a Second Circuit ruling that let New York fire healthcare workers who refused a COVID-19 vaccine on religious grounds. The right to life stands first among the unalienable rights named in the Declaration of Independence, the document that sets the purpose of government as securing life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. Government exists to protect that life through law and order so people can live in peace, as the Preamble puts it when it lists domestic Tranquility as a core reason to form the Union. New York’s mandate forced workers out of hospitals and nursing homes at a time when the state needed every available hand to guard the lives of the sick and elderly. The founders gave Congress power to regulate commerce among the states but never to override the free exercise of religion or to let states punish citizens for obeying their conscience in matters of life and health. Federalist 51 explains that the structure of separated powers and enumerated authority exists to protect individual rights from exactly this kind of local overreach. The First Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1789 to ensure federal courts could hear claims that states had violated fundamental liberties, a practice rooted in the colonial tradition of resisting religious coercion from above. The justices will take up new cases at their long conference on September 28.
