US 10 Venezuelans over ATM malware scheme in Washington

The United States has maintained sanctions under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act since the 1980s to block designated foreign persons and entities from access to the US financial system. The Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control administers those sanctions by adding names to its list and freezing any assets those names hold inside US or in the hands of US persons.

On September 30 2026 the Office of Foreign Assets Control added ten Venezuelan nationals and several companies they control to that list. The department stated the individuals ran a network that installed malware on dozens of automated teller machines to steal card data and cash which the group then laundered through the named companies.

The designations block the ten people and the companies from any transaction that touches the United States or its banks. The move sits inside the broader US effort to disrupt transnational criminal organizations that use stolen funds to finance operations inside and outside Venezuela. The sanctions now stand as the latest entry on the list and turn on whether the named parties hold assets reachable by US banks or partners who comply with the freeze.

Constitutional analysis
The Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control used its authority under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to block property of ten Venezuelan nationals and companies they control. These targets ran an ATM malware operation that drained cash machines and laundered the stolen funds through networks tied to the Tren de Aragua gang. The action freezes any assets the named persons or firms hold under U.S. jurisdiction and bars Americans from dealing with them. The Constitution gives Congress power to regulate commerce with foreign nations, a power the First Congress exercised in the Tariff Act of 1789 when it set duties to protect domestic trade and revenue. Later statutes built on that enumerated authority to let the executive block assets of those who threaten U.S. financial systems. The founders viewed secure property and domestic tranquility as core reasons for government, listed first in the Preamble and protected by the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee that no person shall be deprived of property without due process of law. This sanctions list applies the same principle to foreign actors who steal through digital means. The measure takes effect immediately upon publication in the Federal Register.
Thinking ahead
The US Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control added 10 Venezuelan nationals and the companies they run to its sanctions list on September 30 for running an ATM malware network that drained cash from machines across the US and then moved the stolen dollars through front companies. This is the latest step in a ladder that started with law enforcement takedowns of cash-out crews tied to the Tren de Aragua gang, followed by tracing the malware back to Venezuelan operators who controlled the laundering layer. The sanctions block those named people and firms from the US financial system and freeze any assets they hold here or that touch US banks. How much longer this runs depends on how fast the named targets shift to new shell companies or move laundering routes outside US reach. Observable indicators are fresh OFAC designations and any follow-on Treasury updates naming replacement entities. If no new designations appear in the next two quarters, the pressure on this specific network will likely ease. Next likely move is either expanded sanctions on additional Venezuelan nodes or joint actions with Latin American partners to seize local accounts. The current 2s10s spread of 41 basis points shows no immediate flight-to-safety reaction in Treasuries. Horizon: 6-9 months until next clear rung or measurable drop-off in visible enforcement.
