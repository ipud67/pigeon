Haiti sent 18 suspects to the US to face trial for the murder of president Jovenel Moise five years ago

Haiti has operated without a functioning president since gunmen killed Jovenel Moise in his bedroom in Port-au-Prince in July 2021. The attackers were mostly Colombian former soldiers hired through a Miami security firm. Haitian courts could not move the case forward because gangs control large parts of the country and the justice system has collapsed. US prosecutors took the lead after they determined that the plot was planned and paid for from South Florida as well as the Dominican Republic and Haiti.

On September 21 2026 authorities in Haiti put 18 of the accused on a plane to Miami. The group included 17 Colombian ex-soldiers and Joseph Badio, a former senior official in Haiti's justice ministry who had worked in an anti-corruption unit. They are the latest defendants to reach the Southern District of Florida where 30 people have now been . Four others were convicted there in May on charges of conspiracy to kill or kidnap the president and providing material support for the killing.

The extraditions shift the remaining defendants into US federal court where they face possible life sentences. Haitian investigators continue to operate inside a capital where more than 1,400 people were killed between April and June of this year. The country has set December 13 for its first presidential and legislative elections since 2016 if security allows the vote to take place.

Constitutional analysis
Haiti sent 18 men to Miami on September 21, 2026, to face trial in a U.S. federal court for the 2021 murder of President Jovenel Moise. The right to life stands first among the unalienable rights listed in the Declaration of Independence, and the founders set up government to secure it so citizens could live in safety. The U.S. Constitution gives Congress power to define and punish offenses against the law of nations in Article I, Section 8, Clause 10, and the First Congress used that power in the Crimes Act of 1790 to reach crimes committed abroad when they touched American soil. Prosecutors say the plot was planned and financed from Florida, which placed the case inside U.S. jurisdiction under the protective principle that lets a nation defend itself against attacks hatched on its territory. Thirty suspects have been indicted so far in the Southern District of Florida, four have already been convicted there, and the latest group includes 17 former Colombian soldiers and a former Haitian justice-ministry official. Haiti’s own courts have not delivered justice because gangs control much of the country and earlier probes collapsed. The extradition hands the defendants over to a system built on the rule of law the founders knew was the only sure guard for life and property. A federal trial in Miami will settle whether these men conspired to commit murder on American soil.
