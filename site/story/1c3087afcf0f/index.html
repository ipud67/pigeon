OKLAHOMA
September 24, 2026 · 20:06 UTC

Federal judge says Oklahoma can’t exclude Jewish charter school from program

Oklahoma runs a charter school program that lets private groups open and operate public schools under state oversight and funding. The program has operated for years under a state rule that bars any school operated along religious lines. In March the National Ben Gamla Jewish Charter School Foundation applied to join and was turned down because of that rule, which the state tied to an Oklahoma Supreme Court decision that had blocked religious schools from the program.

On September 24 U.S. District Judge David Russell issued a preliminary against the Oklahoma Statewide Charter School Board. He ruled that a charter school is legally a private institution run by an unelected board rather than a standard public school, so neutrally letting the Jewish school take part does not violate the Establishment Clause. The order blocks the board from enforcing the nonsectarian rule or from keeping the foundation out of the program on account of its religious character.

The foundation can now move forward with its application on the same terms as any other charter operator. The case turns on whether the state must treat the religious school the same as every other applicant once it meets all other requirements.

Constitutional analysis
A federal district judge in Oklahoma issued a preliminary injunction that stops the Statewide Charter School Board from enforcing the state’s nonsectarian requirement against the National Ben Gamla Jewish Charter School Foundation. The judge ruled that a charter school is founded as a private institution with a nonelected board and that neutrally including religious operators in the program does not violate the Establishment Clause. The foundation had sued after the board denied its application, citing an Oklahoma Supreme Court decision that barred religious schools from the charter program. The First Amendment bars Congress from making any law respecting an establishment of religion or prohibiting the free exercise thereof, and the Fourteenth Amendment applies those protections against the states. The founding generation left education to the states and to families; the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 encouraged schools while the First Congress reenacted it without any federal religious test for operators. The judge’s order requires the board to give the Jewish school the same chance to participate that it gives every other private applicant. A final ruling on the merits would settle whether the state’s exclusion policy meets the original public meaning of the Religion Clauses.
Thinking ahead
A federal judge in Oklahoma issued a preliminary injunction Thursday that blocks the statewide charter school board from keeping the National Ben Gamla Jewish Charter School Foundation out of the program over its religious character. The foundation had sued in March after the board turned down its application, citing a state supreme court decision that barred religious schools. The judge wrote that a charter school starts as a private outfit with its own unelected board, so letting it join on the same terms as other charters does not violate the Establishment Clause. This round of the fight has run for six months since the denial and will keep going until the board either drops the nonsectarian rule for this applicant or the case reaches final judgment. The next clear step is either an appeal by the state to the Tenth Circuit or a trial on the permanent injunction. The situation changes only if the Tenth Circuit reverses the injunction, the U.S. Supreme Court takes the case and rules against the school, or the Oklahoma legislature rewrites the charter statute to remove the religious exclusion. The dispute sits on the ladder of charter-school religion cases that turned on whether the school counts as state actor or private operator. Horizon: months to a final district ruling, years if appealed to the Supreme Court.
