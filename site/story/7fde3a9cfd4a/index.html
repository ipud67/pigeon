Trump rejects Iran proposal to reopen Strait of Hormuz

The United States and Israel began strikes on Iran in late February 2026. The fight narrowed to control of the Strait of Hormuz, the 21-mile waterway that carries much of the world's seaborne oil. A June lifted the dual on Iranian ports and the strait and opened a 60-day negotiating window, but that deal collapsed on 8 July and sustained US strikes resumed.

On 26 September 2026, President Trump told reporters at the White House that he had rejected an Iranian proposal delivered through Qatari mediators. The plan, developed in coordination with Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei and presented at the UN General Assembly, called for a full cessation of hostilities across the Middle East, including Lebanon, with the strait reopened on the seventh day of a truce. It required the release of frozen Iranian assets, the lifting of on Iranian oil, and an end to the US naval blockade—the same terms as the collapsed Islamabad Memorandum.

The strait remains largely closed to international shipping. Iranian ports stay under US blockade. Trump said he would like a deal but found the Iranian terms unacceptable, and he posted on social media calling the waterway the Trump Strait. The conflict now turns on whether either side will alter the terms that ended the last negotiating window.

Constitutional analysis
President Trump rejected an Iranian proposal transmitted through Qatari mediators that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz on the seventh day of a truce and end hostilities across the Middle East including Lebanon. The proposal tracked terms from a memorandum of understanding signed earlier this year that called for release of frozen Iranian assets, lifting of sanctions on Iranian oil, and removal of the US naval blockade of Iranian ports. Trump stated at the White House that he would like a deal but found the terms unacceptable because they would open the strait immediately while Iran remains under pressure. The Constitution grants the president authority as commander in chief under Article II, Section 2 to direct military operations and conduct diplomacy with foreign nations. The founders expected the executive to use that power to protect American commerce and keep sea lanes open, as Washington did when he backed the Naval Act of 1794 to build a navy against Barbary and French threats to trade. Federalist 70 explained that energy in the executive allows prompt decisions on war and peace without the delays of committee rule. The first Congress passed the Act to Provide a Naval Armament in 1794 after years of attacks on US shipping, showing the early republic’s view that the president must secure freedom of navigation. Trump’s rejection leaves the current US blockade in place and the strait closed to most international shipping. A formal treaty or congressional statute would settle the exact terms of any lasting agreement on sanctions and naval presence.
Thinking ahead
Trump rejected an Iranian proposal sent through Qatari mediators that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz on the seventh day of a truce and halt fighting across the Middle East including Lebanon. The plan matched terms from the June Islamabad Memorandum that both sides signed then abandoned in July. Trump said the immediate opening of the strait was not acceptable because Iran gets its money from traffic there while US ports remain under blockade. The fight has stayed narrowed to control of the 21-mile strait since the July 12 IRGC move to shut it and the resumption of nightly US strikes. Iranian ports sit under US blockade and Hormuz crossings have stayed low. The proposal came at the UN General Assembly this week with coordination from Iran's supreme leader and followed a meeting between Iran's foreign minister and Trump's envoy. This standoff is likely to run at least through the November US midterm elections. Trump told staff a renewed bombing campaign is the probable next step after that vote. The situation changes only if one side accepts the other's core demand on sanctions, assets, or immediate strait access. Horizon: months, with next US strikes probable after November.
