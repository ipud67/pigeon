Rwanda: Rwanda Eyes Nuclear Tech to Improve Agriculture

Rwanda has spent years building a nuclear science program that includes plans for a power plant and a research reactor. In March the government hosted an IAEA team that reviewed the already in place for a safe nuclear power program. The team listed steps still needed, among them a final national report, updated laws, and formal policies.

On September 16 at the IAEA General Conference in Vienna, Minister of State for Infrastructure Armand Zingiro told delegates that Rwanda now wants to add nuclear and isotopic tools to its farm work. He said the country has expressed interest in the Atoms4Food project run jointly by the IAEA and the UN Food and Agriculture Organization. The project supplies radiation and isotope methods that member states can use on crops, livestock, soil, pests, and food storage.

Rwanda will next sit down with the two agencies to map its own priorities and capacities, then write a national program that matches what it already has. The government has also finished an integrated work plan that spells out how it will carry out the March review’s recommendations and track progress under the IAEA’s Milestones Approach.

Thinking ahead
Rwanda has been building its nuclear infrastructure step by step. In March it hosted an IAEA review mission that looked at its plans for nuclear power and a research reactor. The government then put together an Integrated Work Plan to follow the mission's recommendations. This week at the IAEA General Conference in Vienna the Minister of State for Infrastructure said the country wants to join the Atoms4Food initiative to bring radiation and isotope methods into farming for better yields, pest control, food safety checks, and handling of climate shifts. Officials plan to work with IAEA and FAO teams to map national needs and write a specific program. The push stays tied to the existing work plan and the March review outcomes. Next steps hinge on finishing the national report for a power decision, updating laws, and setting policies. The agricultural angle adds one more item to the Centre for Nuclear Science and Technology but does not change the timeline set by the Integrated Work Plan. This effort will keep moving along the Milestones Approach until the government finishes the legislative review and adopts the full policy package. Without those two markers the program stays in planning. Horizon: at least 18 months on current pace.
