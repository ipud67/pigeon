1d:T6a0,The U.S. Trade Representative brought together officials from more than 50 trading partners in Washington on September 15 2026 for training on how to set up and run import bans on goods made with forced labor. This builds on a ladder that started with the U.S. ban nearly a century ago, got tightened through laws and trade deals in recent years, and led to 12 new adopters by July 2026 — Cambodia, Canada, Ecuador, the European Union, Guatemala, Honduras, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Trinidad and Tobago — after a Section 301 probe. Dozens more countries have since signaled they want the same rules, and the session pairs them with U.S. Customs, Homeland Security, and Labor Department staff for both general training and tailored follow-up help.

The training itself marks the procedural step of turning interest into workable enforcement capacity at foreign borders. Next moves hinge on whether those partners finish drafting their own prohibition rules, stand up inspection systems that can trace supply chains, and begin actual seizures at ports. Observable indicators will be the number of new laws passed, the volume of rejected shipments reported by each country, and formal requests for further U.S. technical assistance.

If fewer than ten partners enact measurable bans within the next two years, or if reported enforcement stays near zero, the expansion stalls. Otherwise the pattern points to wider adoption and joint enforcement.

This setup is likely to run at least another 18 to 24 months as countries move from training to operational rules.
