USTR Convenes Over 50 Trading Partners to Provide Training Related to Imposing and Enforcing Forced Labor Import Prohibitions

The United States has barred the import of goods made with forced labor since the early 1930s. Congress and presidents have tightened that ban in and written it into trade pacts. Those steps, plus a Section 301 investigation by the Office of the United States Trade Representative, led twelve more economies to enact their own import bans by July 2026: Cambodia, Canada, Ecuador, the European Union, Guatemala, Honduras, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Trinidad and Tobago.

On September 15, 2026, the USTR brought together officials from more than fifty trading partners in Washington. Working with the Department of Homeland Security, Customs and Border Protection, and the Department of Labor, it delivered training on how to put such prohibitions in place and enforce them at the border. The session included offers of deeper, country-specific technical help for any government that chooses to act.

Dozens of additional countries have since signaled interest in the same step. The United States continues to supply the training and assistance under the current administration.

Constitutional analysis
The United States has barred the import of goods made with forced labor since the Tariff Act of 1930. On September 15 2026 the Office of the United States Trade Representative used its authority under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 to convene officials from more than 50 trading partners in Washington. The USTR partnered with the Department of Homeland Security Customs and Border Protection and the Department of Labor to deliver training and country-specific technical assistance on how to draft enforce and administer import bans on forced-labor goods at foreign borders. This step rests on the constitutional power of Congress to regulate commerce with foreign nations under Article I Section 8 Clause 3. The first Congress exercised that power in the Tariff Act of 1789 which set duties and opened American ports on terms set by law. Later statutes built on the same enumerated authority to bar goods produced by convict or forced labor. The founders viewed commerce power as a tool to secure the general welfare by protecting free labor and preventing one nation from gaining unfair advantage through coercion of its people. Twelve additional economies Cambodia Canada Ecuador the European Union Guatemala Honduras India Indonesia Mexico Pakistan Sri Lanka and Trinidad and Tobago have now adopted their own forced-labor import prohibitions. Dozens more have asked for help to do the same. The training aims to turn expressed interest into enforceable measures that match the American standard first set nearly a century ago and reinforced by later Congresses and administrations through both domestic law and trade agreements. The operative limit remains the text of the statutes and the agreements. What settles the reach of any new foreign prohibition is the wording each country writes into its own statute and the evidence it requires before blocking a shipment. The United States can share the record of its own enforcement but cannot command sovereign legislatures abroad.
Thinking ahead
The U.S. Trade Representative brought together officials from more than 50 trading partners in Washington on September 15 2026 for training on how to set up and run import bans on goods made with forced labor. This builds on a ladder that started with the U.S. ban nearly a century ago, got tightened through laws and trade deals in recent years, and led to 12 new adopters by July 2026 — Cambodia, Canada, Ecuador, the European Union, Guatemala, Honduras, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Trinidad and Tobago — after a Section 301 probe. Dozens more countries have since signaled they want the same rules, and the session pairs them with U.S. Customs, Homeland Security, and Labor Department staff for both general training and tailored follow-up help. The training itself marks the procedural step of turning interest into workable enforcement capacity at foreign borders. Next moves hinge on whether those partners finish drafting their own prohibition rules, stand up inspection systems that can trace supply chains, and begin actual seizures at ports. Observable indicators will be the number of new laws passed, the volume of rejected shipments reported by each country, and formal requests for further U.S. technical assistance. If fewer than ten partners enact measurable bans within the next two years, or if reported enforcement stays near zero, the expansion stalls. Otherwise the pattern points to wider adoption and joint enforcement. This setup is likely to run at least another 18 to 24 months as countries move from training to operational rules. It heads next toward bilateral assistance packages and joint review meetings to track actual border actions.
