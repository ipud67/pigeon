Use of Certain Restricted Category Aircraft for the Transport of Firefighters for Wildfire Suppression

The Federal Aviation has long barred passengers from riding on restricted-category civil aircraft except for flight crew, trainees, or people performing an essential function tied to the aircraft's special-purpose certification. That rule, spelled out in 14 CFR 91.313(d), also covers operations under Part 119 when the aircraft belongs to a civilian owner. The restriction has kept firefighters who fight fires on the ground from riding those planes to and from wildfire sites unless their presence is required for aerial liquid-dropping work.

On September 14 the FAA published an advance notice of proposed rulemaking that opens the door to changing those sections of the code. The notice follows a direct order in Section 360 of the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024, which requires the agency to evaluate whether firefighters performing ground suppression can be carried on certain restricted-category aircraft. The ANPRM asks for data on the current supply of transport resources, operational barriers that limit their use, and the costs and benefits of allowing the new passenger category.

The docket, FAA-2026-10991, will stay open for public comment before the agency drafts a formal proposed rule. Comments can be submitted through regulations.gov or delivered in person to the FAA's Washington headquarters. The agency has not set a timeline for the next step.

Thinking ahead
The FAA put out an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on September 14 2026 to gather data on moving firefighters by restricted category aircraft for ground wildfire work. This step follows the 2024 FAA Reauthorization Act that ordered the change to sections 91.313 and 119.1. The notice asks for comments on current transport resources, what blocks or helps that transport, and how costs stack up against benefits. The process stays in the comment and data collection phase until the docket closes. FAA will then review submissions to shape a formal proposed rule. The timeline stretches as long as it takes the agency to digest the input and draft the next document; past rules of this type have taken 12 to 24 months from ANPRM to NPRM. If comment volume stays low or shows split views on safety and cost, the FAA could extend the review or scale back the amendment. Strong agreement on workable limits could speed a narrower rule forward. Without enough clear data on operational need and aircraft availability, the agency has already flagged it may stall. Next rung comes when the FAA either publishes a proposed rule or announces it needs more time. Horizon: 12–18 months to a proposed rule.
