WASHINGTON
September 18, 2026 · 19:47 UTC

President Donald J Trump said all 50 state Medicaid programs will get lower drug prices under deals that match the lowest prices paid by other developed nations

Medicaid is the joint federal-state program that pays for health care for low-income Americans. Since the 1960s the federal government has set rules for what states can pay drug makers for medicines covered under the program, while manufacturers have set list prices that often ran higher in the United States than the prices paid by government buyers in other developed countries.

On September 18, 2026, President Trump announced that his administration had reached agreements with pharmaceutical manufacturers requiring them to extend most-favored-nation pricing to all 50 state Medicaid programs. Under the terms, the manufacturers must now charge Medicaid the lowest price they accept from any other developed nation for the same drug.

The change shifts the benchmark for Medicaid reimbursement from domestic list prices to the lowest international rate secured by any developed country. The Council of Economic Advisers calculated that the new pricing structure will reduce federal and state Medicaid drug spending by 64.3 billion dollars over the next ten years while the existing coverage rules for beneficiaries remain unchanged.

Thinking ahead
The Trump administration announced today that it has reached agreements with drug makers to apply most-favored-nation pricing to all 50 state Medicaid programs. Under the deals the lowest price paid by any developed country for a given drug will now set the Medicaid reimbursement rate. The Council of Economic Advisers put the ten-year savings to taxpayers at $64.3 billion while keeping existing coverage rules in place. This is the latest step in a policy sequence that began with earlier Trump-era attempts to tie U.S. prices to foreign benchmarks and continued through legal and industry pushback that slowed but did not cancel the approach. The current move expands the reach from selected drugs or pilot programs to every state Medicaid plan at once. How long it runs depends on whether the agreements hold and on the pace of any court challenges or state-level pushback. Pharmaceutical companies have incentives to renegotiate or litigate specific terms once rebates start hitting their revenue. States and Congress could also alter Medicaid rules or funding formulas in future budget cycles. Next visible steps are the issuance of formal guidance from CMS to states on how to calculate and claim the new rates, followed by the first quarterly reports showing actual price reductions per drug class. The policy stays in force until a court blocks it, a manufacturer walks away from an agreement, or Congress changes the underlying Medicaid statute. Horizon: at least through the current four-year term.
