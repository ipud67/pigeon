With Critical Food Systems Under Attack, Secretary-General Implores Security Council ‘No Time to Lose’ for Breaking Cycle Linking Hunger, Armed Conflict

“Food under Fire: the Security Council’s role in mitigating global and local food crises”
Overview
Following are UN Secretary-General António Guterres’ remarks to the Security Council on conflict and food security — “Food under Fire: the Security Council’s role in mitigating global and local food crises”, today in New York:
Short history
UN Secretary-General António Guterres told the Security Council on August 25, 2026 that there is no time to lose in breaking the cycle between hunger and armed conflict. The link between war and food shortages has grown since the early 2000s as fighting in places like Syria, Yemen, and South Sudan destroyed farms, blocked trade routes, and cut off aid. In 2015 the Security Council began passing resolutions that recognized this connection and called on warring parties to allow food deliveries. By 2018 the Council adopted Resolution 2417, which required parties in armed conflict to stop using starvation as a method of warfare and to permit safe passage of food and medical supplies. Today the pattern continues in active wars in Ukraine, Gaza, Sudan, and the Sahel, where attacks on farms, grain stores, ports, and transport have sharply reduced food output and driven up prices worldwide. Guterres spoke at the New York meeting to press the Council to act on that record.
Predictive model
The UN Secretary-General told the Security Council today that attacks on food systems are feeding a cycle of hunger and fighting with no quick fix in sight. This is the latest call in a string of similar briefings the Council has heard over the past two years as conflicts in places like Sudan, Gaza, and Ukraine keep wrecking farms, silos, and supply routes. Guterres pushed for concrete Council action on humanitarian access, protection of civilian infrastructure, and better early-warning systems. How much longer this runs depends on whether member states turn the speech into a resolution or working group with teeth. Right now the observable indicators are thin: another high-level meeting, repeated warnings, and no announced follow-up vote or sanctions mechanism. Without a procedural step like a draft resolution tabled or a dedicated monitoring mandate approved, the cycle described today is likely to grind on for years in the active conflict zones. Next rung would be any Council member putting forward specific language on food-security safeguards or an Arria-formula meeting that actually produces agreed language. If those stay missing, the pattern of speeches without measurable enforcement continues. Horizon: at least 12-24 months of repeated warnings unless a binding Council product appears.
