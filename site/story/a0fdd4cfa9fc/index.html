EPA repeals Biden-era emission rules for coal and fossil-fueled-fired power plants

The Clean Air Act has required the Environmental Protection Agency to set performance standards for new and existing stationary sources of air pollution since 1970. In 2009 the agency issued an endangerment finding that listed greenhouse gases as pollutants subject to those standards. That opened the way for the 2024 Carbon Pollution Standards, which set limits on coal-fired and new natural-gas-fired power plants. Plants had to capture 90 percent of their carbon dioxide emissions from the smokestack or stop operating.

On September 14 the EPA issued a final rule that removes those 2024 limits entirely. Administrator Lee Zeldin announced the repeal in Houston at the G20 Energy Abundance meeting together with the interior and energy secretaries. The agency also cited its February rescission of the 2009 endangerment finding as the legal basis for ending the of power-plant greenhouse gases.

The rule eliminates the compliance obligations that had applied to the plants. Industry groups say the prior standards had pushed existing coal units into early retirement and blocked construction of new gas units at a time when electricity demand is rising. Environmental organizations and several states have already signaled they will file suit to block the repeal. The standards on vehicle tailpipe emissions that rested on the same 2009 finding remain under separate review.

Constitutional analysis
The Environmental Protection Agency repealed the 2024 Carbon Pollution Standards that required coal-fired and new gas-fired power plants to capture 90 percent of smokestack emissions or shut down. This action removes more than 300 billion dollars in projected industry compliance costs and ends a rule that rural electric cooperatives called unlawful, unrealistic, and unachievable because it forced early retirements of existing plants and blocked new natural gas capacity at a time of rising electricity demand. The Constitution grants the federal government only enumerated powers. The Clean Air Act rests on the Commerce Clause, yet the founders designed that clause to regulate trade among the states, not to let one agency dictate the nation’s entire energy supply. Federalist 45 lists the powers “few and defined” that belong to the national government; dictating how power plants generate electricity for every home and factory falls outside those bounds. The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states and the people all powers not delegated to the United States. Energy production and the reliability of the electric grid have always been matters of local knowledge and state authority, not distant bureaucratic command. The repeal returns that responsibility closer to the level where the founding generation expected most governance to occur. The right to life depends first on ordered liberty secured by limited government. When rules threaten to extinguish reliable baseload power, they endanger hospitals, water treatment, and the domestic tranquility the Preamble names as a core purpose of the Union. Washington himself led militia to enforce federal law in the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794, but he never claimed power to reorder every trade or manufacture in the states. The 1789 Tariff Act and the First Bank debate show the founders arguing over the reach of federal commerce power. Those debates settled on narrow construction so that government would not suffocate the productive energies of a free people. Repealing the emission mandate aligns with that tradition by refusing to regulate an entire industry out of existence through administrative fiat. Legal challenges will test the move, but the constitutional measure remains the enumerated powers, the Tenth Amendment, and the founding preference for energy abundance over centralized control.
Predictive model
EPA finalized the repeal of the 2024 Carbon Pollution Standards on September 14 in Houston. The move follows the February rescission of the 2009 Greenhouse Gas Endangerment Finding that had grounded the earlier rules. Zeldin and cabinet officials framed the action as removing unworkable mandates that raised compliance costs and threatened grid reliability. Legal challenges from environmental groups and Democratic-led states will start immediately. Court filings will test whether the current EPA reading of the Clean Air Act holds up. Congressional Democrats have already signaled they will push oversight hearings and possible legislation to restore the limits. The rule stays in force unless a federal court blocks it or a later administration reverses it again. Rural electric cooperatives say the change lets existing plants keep running while demand grows. Next phase turns on how fast suits reach the appellate courts and whether any stay is granted. Horizon: 12-18 months until first appellate rulings.
