Promoting Fair Competition In Livestock Markets And Expanding Market Access for American Meat Producers

Overview
By the authority vested in me as President by the Constitution and the laws of the United States of America, it is hereby ordered: Section 1. Purpose. American ranchers want to be able to butcher, process, package, and sell their meat to consumers across State lines while maintaining the highest standards of food safety and avoid
Short history
On February 6 2026 President Trump issued 11010. It directed federal agencies to review rules on cattle imports slaughterhouse operations and beef supply chains. The order required agencies to report back with steps that would cut costs passed to ranchers and processors. On August 26 2026 the president signed a follow-up proclamation titled Further Ensuring Affordable Beef for the American Consumer. It extended the review period and added requirements for faster approval of new meat-packing facilities and streamlined cattle movement across state lines. The latest proclamation keeps the same powers in place. It orders the Department of Agriculture and other agencies to continue the work started in February and to send updated compliance reports by the end of 2026.
Constitutional analysis
The right to life begins with the food that sustains it, and the founders saw free commerce in meat and livestock as part of the domestic tranquility the Preamble lists first among the reasons to form a government. This executive order directs federal agencies to open interstate markets so ranchers can butcher, process, package, and sell their own meat across state lines under existing food-safety rules. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to regulate commerce among the states, yet the founders expected that power to promote, not choke, the trade of farmers and drovers who fed the new republic. Washington himself backed the Tariff Act of 1789 and the Militia Acts to secure the economic lifeblood of free citizens on the land. The order steps into a thicket of federal rules that have long funneled most livestock through a handful of large processors, a concentration the first Congress would have viewed as hostile to the independent yeoman the republic was built to protect. Federalism still leaves room for states to set their own health standards, but the national government must not use its commerce power to lock small producers out of national markets their ancestors helped settle. The measure returns policy closer to the founding expectation that government exists to clear paths for honest labor rather than stand in them.
Predictive model
The White House issued a new proclamation on August 26 that builds directly on the February 6 version. Both documents cite the same role of U.S. cattle ranchers in food supply and direct federal agencies to keep beef costs down for consumers. The latest step keeps the existing policy framework in place without adding new mandates or deadlines. This setup holds until the current administration changes course or a court blocks part of the directive. No expiration date appears in the text, so the policy runs until a future proclamation revokes it or Congress passes a law that overrides it. Observable indicators are thin right now—no new lawsuits filed, no agency compliance reports released, no congressional hearings scheduled. Next move likely sits with federal agencies named in the original order as they file routine updates on their actions. Without fresh litigation or a shift in administration priorities the proclamation stays on the books. Horizon estimate: At least through the end of the current term unless a court ruling or new proclamation appears first.
