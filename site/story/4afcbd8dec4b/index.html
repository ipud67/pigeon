South Korea’s top diplomat to meet Rubio amid US investment, Hormuz discussions

Foreign Minister Cho Hyun will visit Washington from Sept 17 to 19.

Constitutional analysis
South Korea’s foreign minister travels to Washington this week to meet Secretary of State Marco Rubio. The talks center on honoring the 2025 trade agreement in which Seoul pledged $350 billion in U.S. investments in exchange for Washington holding tariffs on Korean goods at 15 percent. The Constitution gives Congress the power to regulate commerce with foreign nations under Article I, Section 8, Clause 3, a power the founders used quickly in the Tariff Act of 1789 to set duties that protected American industry while generating revenue. That early statute rested on the idea that government exists first to secure the safety and prosperity of the people so they can live in peace, the very purpose stated in the Preamble’s call for domestic Tranquility and the common defense. The meeting also covers possible South Korean naval help to keep the Strait of Hormuz open for trade. The founders viewed free navigation of the seas as vital to American commerce and therefore to national survival; Washington enforced this with the Naval Act of 1794 and the Quasi-War against French privateers who seized U.S. ships. Any deployment decision belongs to the elected leaders of both nations under their own constitutional forms, not to permanent bureaucracies or international pressure. North Korea policy will likely surface as well, with talk of another Trump-Kim meeting. The first duty of the federal government remains the protection of American lives and territory, a principle that guided the founders when they raised militia under the Militia Acts of 1792 to put down the Whiskey Rebellion and secure the new republic’s borders.
Thinking ahead
South Korea’s foreign minister heads to Washington next week for talks with Secretary of State Marco Rubio. The meeting sits on the ladder of last year’s trade deal that locked in $350 billion in Korean investment commitments in exchange for a 15 percent tariff cap. Industry Minister Kim said on Sept 13 that negotiators hoped to wrap the final investment terms and announce them this week, yet several items remain open and the parliamentary briefing on those plans was pushed back from Sept 17. The Hormuz question runs in parallel: Seoul is still reviewing options for naval support after Trump pressed for more help securing the strait. North Korea will sit on the agenda too, given reports that Trump wants a summit with Kim as soon as this fall. Cho’s three-day visit ends Sept 19; the South Korean president holds his own news conference the same day. This round of talks will probably run through the rest of September while the investment details get settled and Seoul decides on Hormuz assets. Next visible steps are any joint readout after the Sept 18 meeting and whatever the president says publicly that afternoon. If the investment package stays stuck or Seoul holds off on Hormuz, the friction carries into October. Horizon: through end of September at least.
