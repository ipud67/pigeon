MINNESOTA
September 17, 2026 · 09:28 UTC

ICE agent charged in Minneapolis shooting taken into custody after Texas standoff

The confrontation happened on January 14 during Operation Metro Surge, the federal immigration enforcement push ordered under President Trump that sent ICE teams into Minneapolis and set off weeks of street protests and clashes with agents. In one apartment raid that day, agent Christian Castro shot Venezuelan national Julio Cesar Sosa-Celis in the leg through a closed door while children were inside the unit. Castro then told Homeland Security superiors that the man and others had come at him with a snow shovel and broom; body-camera video showed otherwise.

Minnesota prosecutors filed state assault and false-report charges. When Castro returned to his home state of Texas after the shooting, officials there held him for ninety days on a governor’s warrant. Texas Governor Greg Abbott declined to sign extradition papers sent by Minnesota Governor Tim Walz, citing questions over whether Castro counted as a fugitive once the Department of Homeland Security had reassigned him home. A federal judge in Texas ordered his release on August 27 after he posted bond on separate federal charges of lying to investigators.

On September 17 Castro drove north under an agreement with Minnesota authorities to surrender at his lawyer’s office in downtown Minneapolis. Agents from the Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension took him into custody minutes before the scheduled handoff and booked him into Hennepin County jail on the outstanding state warrant. The case now rests on the state charges moving forward in Minnesota court.

Constitutional analysis
An ICE agent stands charged in Minnesota with assault for shooting a man in the leg through an apartment door while children were inside. The right to life comes first. Government exists to secure that right and the domestic tranquility the Preamble names, so officers must answer for force that wounds civilians. The Constitution gives states power to enforce their own criminal laws under the Tenth Amendment and the extradition clause in Article IV, Section 2. Federalist 42 explains that the clause prevents one state from becoming a refuge for those who break another state’s peace. Minnesota issued its warrant, Texas held the man for ninety days, and the standoff ended when he reached Minneapolis on September 17 and was booked into Hennepin County jail. The founders expected officers of the law to face local juries when video evidence contradicted their reports of the event. Federal reassignment after the January shooting does not erase the state’s authority to try the case.
Thinking ahead
Castro turned himself in under an agreement with Minnesota authorities after eight months of back and forth that started when he returned to Texas right after the January 14 shooting. Texas held him for 90 days on a governor's refusal to extradite, then released him in late August once a federal judge in McAllen turned down Minnesota's request. The state assault and false-reporting charges now sit in Hennepin County court following his booking there Wednesday. The case moves to pretrial hearings and possible motions on the video evidence that undercut his initial account. Prosecutors must show the shooting through the apartment door met the legal threshold for assault while children were inside. Castro's lawyers have signaled they will contest the charges and seek dismissal or acquittal at trial. This phase runs until a plea or jury verdict. Next observable step is the arraignment date and any bond conditions set by the Minnesota judge. If the federal false-statement case in Texas produces new evidence it could alter the state timeline, but no such filing has appeared yet. Horizon: six to twelve months to trial or settlement.
