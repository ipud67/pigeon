DOJ Revises Justice Manual to Strengthen False Claims Act Enforcement

The False Claims Act has let private whistleblowers and the federal government sue anyone who submits false bills to Medicare since Congress strengthened the law in 1986. Capstone Diagnostics ran a clinical laboratory in Atlanta that performed genetic tests and respiratory panels paid for by the program. For years the company relied on sales staff and standing orders to move large volumes of tests through Medicare without fresh review by the doctor who saw each patient.

On September 17 2026 the Justice Department filed a complaint in federal court in Austin against lab executives Jay Johnson and Austin Whiles. The suit charges that the two ran one scheme at church health fairs and religious conferences where staff swabbed people then filled in physician names signatures and standing orders to bill Medicare for genetic tests. A second scheme used senior living facilities that needed COVID-19 testing to trigger broader respiratory panels through copied signatures preset diagnosis codes and orders entered by salespeople instead of treating doctors. The complaint also names Johnson’s former wife Sarah Haslock and several companies that received money traced to the tests and accuses Whiles of routing millions in secret volume commissions to a firm he controlled.

The government has already settled with Capstone and its owner Drew Maloney for 14.3 million dollars and with the lab’s billing company VitalAxis for 300 479 dollars. Johnson faces a separate criminal returned in December 2025 in the Northern District of Georgia on conspiracy to commit health care fraud tied to the church events; that case remains pending. The civil suit continues under the False Claims Act’s qui tam provisions with the Civil Division and the U.S. Attorney’s Office in Atlanta handling it alongside the HHS inspector general.

Constitutional analysis
The federal government exists first to secure the unalienable right to life, as the Declaration states, and to provide for the general welfare by guarding public money raised through taxes on the people. The Justice Department sued two lab executives in Atlanta for schemes that allegedly funneled millions in Medicare payments for unnecessary genetic and respiratory tests ordered through kickbacks at church events and senior living facilities. The complaint claims they used copied physician signatures, standing orders, and sales staff instead of real doctor judgment to trigger the claims. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to lay and collect taxes and to provide for the general welfare, but the founders understood this as limited spending on objects truly common to all, not open-ended programs ripe for fraud. The Federalist No. 41 warned that loose construction of the spending power would swallow every other limit and turn the government into an unlimited one. Medicare, created by statute in 1965, operates under that power, yet the alleged fraud shows how easily such programs invite abuse when kickbacks replace individualized medical decisions. The founders knew fraud against the public purse struck at the consent of the governed. Washington led troops to put down the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 when citizens refused a tax they saw as unjustly collected and spent. The First Congress passed the Tariff Act of 1789 to raise revenue honestly and the Militia Acts to secure domestic tranquility. Suing to recover funds taken through false claims follows that tradition of protecting the treasury that funds the government's core duties, starting with the protection of life and property. The case rests on the False Claims Act, rooted in Civil War-era efforts to stop contractors from cheating the government. It seeks recovery from the executives, the ex-wife who allegedly received transferred funds, and related entities. Earlier settlements with the lab and its billing company totaled over $14 million. A separate criminal indictment against one executive for health-care fraud remains pending.
Thinking ahead
DOJ filed a civil complaint in federal court against Jay Johnson and Austin Whiles, the two executives who ran Capstone Diagnostics in Atlanta. The suit claims they ran two kickback schemes that sent millions in Medicare claims for genetic tests at church events and respiratory panels in senior living facilities, with paperwork signed by doctors who never saw the patients. The government already settled with the lab itself and its owner for $14.3 million and with the billing company for $300,000. Johnson faces a separate criminal indictment from December 2025 on conspiracy to commit health care fraud tied to the church side; that case is still pending. The civil complaint also names his ex-wife Sarah Haslock and several companies as targets for money-recovery claims. The case sits at the complaint stage, so the next visible steps are service on the defendants, their answer or motion to dismiss, and whatever discovery the government turns over from the earlier qui tam filing. How long it runs depends on whether the parties settle fast like the lab did or fight every document request and expert report. The criminal track against Johnson moves on its own schedule in the Northern District of Georgia; a conviction there would hand the civil side a strong collateral-estoppel card. Without more filings or a trial date on the record, the indicators stay thin. Horizon: 18 to 36 months to resolution or settlement.
