Modern Orthodox Jewish day schools planning to pray outside NYC mayor's residence on Friday

Modern Orthodox Jewish day schools in New York City have operated under rising reports of antisemitic incidents since at least the summer. Mayor Zohran Mamdani took office on January 1. In his first month anti-Jewish hate crimes rose 182 percent from the January figure the year before. The schools have continued regular classes and prayer services on their own grounds while tracking those numbers.

On Wednesday a reporter asked Mamdani about the planned gathering. He said the students could hold their prayer service or vigil outside Gracie Mansion and that he would be elsewhere in the city at the time. Rabbi Binyamin Krauss of SAR Academy and Rabbi Joshua Lookstein of Ramaz confirmed the permit allows up to 500 people. Krauss expects about 240 students from SAR and Ramaz on Friday morning. Lookstein plans to bring roughly 140 students from eighth and eleventh grades at Ramaz. Worshipers from Congregation Kehilath Jeshurun will also attend. SAR is sending its eighth graders plus some from ninth and tenth.

The schools will conduct the Friday morning prayer service outside the mayor’s residence on September 19. Krauss described the choice of location as deliberate. Lookstein said the message is that the Jewish community feels vulnerable and expects the mayor to address that. The event now turns on how many additional schools join and how city officials manage the permitted gathering.

Thinking ahead
Modern Orthodox Jewish day schools in New York have scheduled a prayer service outside Mayor Mamdani’s residence for Friday morning, with SAR Academy sending about 240 students from 8th through 10th grades and Ramaz planning to bring roughly 140 from 8th and 11th grades. Organizers obtained a police permit for up to 500 people and described the gathering as a show of presence, Jewish identity, support for Israel, and a call for greater safety after antisemitic incidents this summer. Mayor Mamdani responded at a Wednesday press conference that he welcomes the event but will be elsewhere in the city at the time. This fits the pattern of Jewish groups in the city turning to visible public prayer and statements since Mamdani took office on January 1. The schools have framed the action as teaching students to show up in public rather than depending on a direct meeting with the mayor. The event is set for one morning and carries no announced follow-on dates. It will likely end after the service unless additional schools join and organizers convert it into a weekly or monthly rhythm, which would require new permits and sustained turnout. Without a shift in city policy on hate-crime enforcement or a change in mayor, the underlying tension stays in place and similar one-off gatherings can recur when incidents spike. Next rung depends on turnout numbers Friday and whether city officials log any policy response in the following week. Horizon: one day.
