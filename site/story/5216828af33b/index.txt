1a:T66c,The Constitution was written first to secure the right to life. The Preamble lists “domestic Tranquility” and the common defense as core reasons for forming the Union, because government exists to stop men from slaughtering one another. Sudan’s civil war has already killed tens of thousands of civilians; letting arms flow freely to both sides only feeds more killing. The founders knew that peace at home requires order, and order sometimes means denying weapons to those who use them on innocents. Washington himself led the militia to put down the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794, showing that the federal government must act when violence threatens the peace the people consented to.

Yet the United States has no enumerated power to police the internal armed conflicts of foreign nations. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to regulate commerce with foreign nations and to declare war, but not to run the world’s police force through the UN. The founders warned against permanent alliances that would drag America into every quarrel (Federalist 6 and Washington’s Farewell Address). An arms embargo covering an entire sovereign country is a sanction and a use of American diplomatic power that should be judged by whether it truly serves the safety of our own people first.

Expanding a twenty-year regional embargo to all of Sudan may slow the slaughter, but it must rest on clear constitutional ground: the right to life at home, the enumerated foreign-affairs powers, and the consent of the governed. 1d:T56c,The US push to widen the Darfur arms embargo to the whole country sits on a ladder that began with the original 2004 Darfur-only restrictions and has seen repeated short extensions as fighting spread. Monday's proposal sets a direct procedural next step: a Security Council vote before the current measures lapse in September 2026. Russia has already signaled it will block anything that restricts the Sudanese army, which anchors the likely outcome as a narrow renewal limited to Darfur rather than nationwide expansion.

How much longer this drags on depends on whether Russia and China keep veto leverage or if African Council members peel away. Observable indicators are the exact wording of any draft resolution tabled in the next few weeks and the public statements from the Sudanese armed forces and the Rapid Support Forces on outside arms supplies. If the Sudanese army steps up attacks in Darfur or if RSF forces cross into Chad or South Sudan, pressure for broader sanctions will tick up; absent those crossings or battlefield shifts, the embargo stays narrow.

Next rung is the closed-door Council negotiations that usually follow a US draft. Without a clear shift in Russian posture or a major new atrocity report that changes African votes, the expansion stalls.
